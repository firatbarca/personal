My degree was in political science, and the question that first pulled me towards this field was not about carbon accounting at all. It was about people — specifically, what happens to them when the place they call home stops being liveable. Years later, working in ESG and non-financial reporting, I keep returning to that question, because it sits in an awkward gap. Climate change is measured meticulously in tonnes of CO₂, but the human movement it drives is still, for most organisations, a footnote. This summer's heatwaves across southern Europe and the Mediterranean have made that gap harder to ignore.

## Mobility is a climate outcome, not a side issue

We are used to thinking about climate impacts in physical terms: floods, droughts, crop failures, rising seas. Human mobility is what happens next. When a region can no longer support the livelihoods it once did, people adapt in the most human way possible — they move. Sometimes that means a permanent relocation across a border; far more often it means something quieter and internal, such as a farming family sending one member to a city for work, or a coastal community drifting inland one household at a time.

The scale is not speculative. The World Bank's *Groundswell* modelling has long suggested that, on current trajectories, well over 200 million people could be moving within their own countries by 2050 for climate-related reasons. What matters for anyone working in sustainability is that these are not distant, abstract figures. They describe the labour forces, supply chains, and communities that businesses already depend on today.

## Where this touches the "S" and the "G"

ESG frameworks are surprisingly comfortable talking about the environmental drivers of migration and surprisingly quiet about the movement itself. That is the blind spot. Human mobility lives squarely in the **social** and **governance** dimensions, and it shows up in ways that are entirely material:

- **Supply-chain resilience.** Agricultural and manufacturing supply chains rest on workers who are among the most exposed to heat stress, water scarcity, and displacement. A supplier map that ignores climate mobility is describing a snapshot, not a forecast.
- **Labour and human rights.** People who move under pressure are more vulnerable to exploitation, informal contracts, and unsafe conditions. Under frameworks like the CSRD and the ESRS **S2** standard on workers in the value chain, that vulnerability is not charity — it is a disclosable risk.
- **Community relations.** Sites in receiving regions face pressure on housing, water, and services. Sites in departing regions face a shrinking, ageing workforce. Both belong in a serious materiality assessment.

None of this requires a company to solve migration. It requires it to *see* migration as part of the operating environment, the same way it already sees an emissions factor or an energy price.

## Why reporting is the right lever

I am a reporting person by trade, so I will make the reporting case plainly. The value of a disclosure regime is not the document at the end; it is the discipline it imposes upstream. When a standard forces you to name a risk, assign an owner, and gather evidence, the organisation is quietly compelled to understand something it might otherwise have avoided.

That is exactly what human mobility needs right now. Treated as a vague humanitarian concern, it stays outside the boardroom. Framed as a material factor in a double-materiality assessment — with an owner, a metric, and a trail — it enters the same conversation as every other risk that gets managed rather than merely lamented.

> The environment tells us *what* is changing. Human mobility tells us *who* it changes things for. A framework that measures the first without the second is only doing half the job.

## A modest place to start

I am wary of grand programmes that never survive their first budget cycle. So my suggestion is deliberately small. When you next run a materiality assessment, add one question to the workshop: *where are our people, our suppliers, and our communities most exposed to climate-driven movement — and what would it cost us if we were wrong?* You will not have a complete answer. You may not have a good one at all the first time. But you will have started treating mobility as data rather than as background noise, and that shift in posture is where every serious disclosure begins.

Climate change was never only about the atmosphere. It was always, eventually, about people and where they can build a life. The organisations that grasp this early will not just report more honestly — they will plan more wisely. And in a warming decade, planning wisely may turn out to be the most valuable form of resilience we have.
