There is a conversation happening in a lot of mid-sized companies right now that goes roughly: we are below the new thresholds, so this is behind us.

The first half is often correct. The second half depends entirely on who your customers are.

## Two different exits

The Omnibus raised CSRD scope thresholds to more than 1,000 employees and more than 450 million euro in turnover. It entered into force on 18 March 2026, with Member States given twelve months to transpose the CSRD provisions and first application required for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027. The stated intent included limiting the trickle-down of obligations onto smaller companies, which was a real problem worth addressing.

But falling out of direct scope has two quite different meanings depending on where you sit.

If your customers are consumers, you have genuinely exited a regime. The obligation is gone and little replaces it.

If your customers are large corporates that remain in scope, you have exited the regime and stayed in their value chain. Their disclosure obligations still require them to account for upstream emissions and impacts. They will still come to you for data. What changed is the channel: the request now arrives as a procurement requirement rather than a legal one.

> Leaving the regulation does not remove you from someone else's reporting boundary.

## Why the procurement channel is harder

A regulatory obligation, whatever else can be said about it, is at least standardised. Everyone in scope faces the same requirements, expressed in the same standards, on the same timetable. That standardisation is worth something.

A procurement request has none of those properties. Different customers ask in different formats, on different cycles, with different definitions and different levels of granularity. Answering ten customers can mean building ten answers to a question that is substantially the same underneath.

That fragmentation is a real cost, and it lands hardest on exactly the companies the simplification was meant to protect.

## The voluntary standard is the partial answer

The most useful development here got less attention than it deserved. Alongside the revised ESRS adopted on 3 July 2026, the European Commission adopted a separate delegated act establishing a voluntary sustainability reporting standard for undertakings outside CSRD scope.

A voluntary standard does not create an obligation. What it creates is a common reference point, which is the thing the procurement channel currently lacks. A supplier that reports against it can point ten customers at one answer instead of building ten. A customer receiving it knows what definitions were used.

I would expect standards like this to become expected through commercial pressure rather than legislation. Not because anyone mandates them, but because the alternative for both sides is worse.

## What I would do if I were below the threshold

Three things, none of them expensive.

**Find out whether your customers are in scope.** This determines everything else. It is a short exercise and most companies have not done it explicitly.

**Keep the capability, drop the volume.** The validation routines, the ownership model, the documentation habit: these took time to build and are the genuinely hard part. Maintaining a smaller data set with those controls intact is far cheaper than rebuilding the controls later. What you can safely stop doing is producing disclosure volume nobody is asking for.

**Use the voluntary standard as the format.** Answering in a recognised structure is more efficient than answering in whatever shape each request arrives in, and it positions you well if procurement expectations converge on it.

## The wider point

I wrote when the Omnibus landed that [it raised the threshold rather than the ambition](/blog/omnibus-raised-threshold-not-ambition/). This is the supplier-side version of the same observation.

Simplification redistributed the obligation. It did not remove the demand for the information, because the demand was never really coming from the regulation. It was coming from the fact that large organisations need to understand their value chains, and that need is not sensitive to where a threshold sits.

Companies that read their exit from scope as an exit from the subject will find the requests keep arriving, in less convenient forms, from people with commercial leverage rather than legal authority. That is a harder negotiation than the one they think they have left behind.
