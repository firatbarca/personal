Double materiality asks two questions at once. How does the organisation affect the world, and how do sustainability issues affect the organisation's financial position. Answering either one properly requires evidence. Deciding what the evidence means requires judgment, and the two are easy to confuse.

Tooling has become genuinely good at the first part. Scanning stakeholder input, clustering topics, mapping value chains, surfacing what peers disclose: these are pattern-recognition tasks and they compress well. The time savings are real and I use them.

What I have not seen is a reduction in the part that actually determines the outcome.

## The three decisions no tool makes for you

Strip a materiality assessment down and there are three moments where a person decides something that cannot be derived from the inputs.

**Where the threshold sits.** Every assessment produces a ranked list of topics with scores attached. Somewhere on that list is a line, and topics above it get reported while topics below it do not. Nothing in the data tells you where to draw the line. That is a judgment about risk appetite, stakeholder expectation, and what the organisation is prepared to stand behind.

**What the scores mean.** A topic scoring highly on stakeholder concern and low on financial impact is a genuine question, not a calculation. Resolving it requires a view about the time horizon over which impacts become financial, which is a strategic position rather than an analytical output.

**What gets excluded and why.** Exclusions are the part of an assessment most likely to be questioned later, and the part least supported by tooling. A topic left out needs a reason that will still look reasonable in three years, written by someone willing to be associated with it.

> A tool can tell you what the evidence contains. It cannot tell you what you are prepared to defend.

## The risk of well-presented output

There is a specific failure mode I have come to watch for, and it is not that the tool produces something wrong.

It is that the tool produces something that looks finished. A clean matrix, sensibly clustered topics, defensible-looking scores. The output arrives with an implicit authority that a rough draft would not have, and the judgment questions get skipped because the artefact does not look like it is waiting for them.

I have written before about [why fluent output is not the same as verified output](/blog/ai-in-esg-reporting-assistant-not-author/). Materiality is where that gap does the most damage, because the assessment determines the entire scope of what follows. An error in a single emissions figure affects one number. An error in materiality shapes the whole report, and it propagates into the next cycle as a baseline.

## What this means under the revised standards

This is more consequential now than it was two years ago, not less.

The European Commission adopted the revised ESRS on 3 July 2026, cutting mandatory datapoints by more than 60% and total datapoints by more than 70%, applying for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027. A shorter standard does not remove the need to determine what is material. If anything it raises the stakes, because with fewer datapoints there is less surrounding material to dilute a scoping error.

CSRD also continues to require limited assurance from the first year of application. An assurance provider examining a materiality assessment will want to understand how conclusions were reached, not just what they were. "The analysis indicated" is a considerably weaker answer than "we concluded, on this basis, and here is the record of the discussion."

## Keeping the judgment visible

The practical response is not to use fewer tools. It is to keep a clear line between what was analysed and what was decided.

In practice that means recording the decisions separately from the analysis: where the threshold was set and why, which topics were contested and how the disagreement resolved, what was excluded and on what basis, and who signed off. A short document, written at the time, naming people.

Analysis compresses well and should be compressed. Accountability does not compress, and attempts to make it look as though it has tend to be discovered at the least convenient moment.

The most useful thing tooling has done for materiality assessments, in my experience, is give the people responsible more time to spend on the questions that were always the hard part. That is a real gain. It is a different gain from the one usually advertised.
