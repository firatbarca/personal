There is a version of climate migration that turns up in headlines every summer: vast columns of people crossing continents, driven from their homes by a warming planet, arriving somewhere else all at once. It is a vivid picture, and it is mostly wrong. My first academic love was political science, and the question that has stayed with me since is a quieter one — how a changing climate reshapes where and how people can live. After a decade working around sustainability, I have come to think we discuss climate and human mobility in exactly the wrong register: as a border problem, when it is really a question of development, dignity, and adaptation.

This summer has made the subject hard to ignore. Another run of record heat, another set of floods that displaced families for weeks rather than headlines for days. So it is worth being precise about what the evidence actually says, and why it matters for anyone — businesses included — trying to plan for the decade ahead.

## Most movement never crosses a border

The first correction is the most important. The great majority of climate-related movement is internal: people relocating within their own country, often over short distances, and frequently for a season rather than for good. The World Bank's Groundswell work has long estimated that internal climate migration could reach well over a hundred million people by 2050 across the most exposed regions — and that is movement inside national boundaries, not toward wealthy ones.

The second correction is that mobility is rarely a single dramatic event. It is a spectrum. A farmer whose harvest fails may send one family member to a nearby city for wage work. A coastal household may move a few kilometres inland after a storm and return when they can. Some people cannot move at all — the poorest are often "trapped populations", too short of resources to relocate even when staying has become dangerous. Reducing all of this to a picture of foreign arrivals misses almost everyone it claims to describe.

> Climate change rarely forces a clean choice between staying and leaving. It narrows people's options until moving becomes the least bad one.

## Why a sustainability professional should care

It would be easy to file this under humanitarian concern and move on. I would push back on that. Human mobility sits much closer to the core of corporate sustainability than most reporting frameworks yet admit.

- **Supply chains run through exposed places.** The agricultural regions, coastlines, and low-income labour markets most affected by climate mobility are the same ones that feed global supply chains. Workforce disruption is a business risk, not only a social one.
- **The "S" in ESG is where this lives.** Social disclosure has always been the weakest leg of ESG. Migration, labour conditions, and community resilience are precisely the material issues a serious social strategy should engage with.
- **Adaptation finance needs direction.** As money slowly flows toward climate adaptation, someone has to decide what to fund. Planning that ignores how people actually move — locally, seasonally, reluctantly — will fund the wrong things.

None of this asks a company to solve displacement. It asks planners to stop treating the people in their value chain as a fixed backdrop that will always be where it was last year.

## What a serious response looks like

The encouraging news is that the policy conversation has matured. Frameworks increasingly recognise climate mobility as something to be managed with dignity, rather than merely prevented at a fence. For organisations, a credible stance is less complicated than it sounds:

- Map where your operations and suppliers overlap with climate-exposed, mobility-prone regions — and treat that as material risk data, not charity.
- Support adaptation that helps people stay safely where they wish to, and move with dignity where they must.
- Design workforce and community programmes for a labour force that may be more mobile than the org chart assumes.

Above all, drop the language of invasion. People who move because the climate has narrowed their choices are not a threat to be contained; they are, more often than not, someone's employee, supplier, or customer adjusting to a world that shifted under them.

## Keeping people in the frame

I came to sustainability through politics, and politics is finally the point. Emissions curves and disclosure templates are the machinery of climate work, but they exist in service of something human: whether people can keep a livelihood, a home, and a measure of choice as the physical world changes around them. Human mobility is where the abstraction of "climate risk" becomes a person with a suitcase and a difficult decision. The least we can do — as citizens, and as professionals who claim sustainability as our trade — is to see them clearly, and plan as if they count.
