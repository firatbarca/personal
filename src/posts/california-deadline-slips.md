On 24 June 2026, the California Air Resources Board announced it intended to defer the first greenhouse gas reporting deadline under SB 253, the Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act, from 10 August 2026 to 10 November 2026. The change is being made through an expedited rulemaking process including a 15-day public comment period, and takes effect subject to approval by California's Office of Administrative Law. CARB also indicated it would propose limited changes to clarify certain requirements.

Worth checking the current status before relying on any of that, since the process was still running when it was announced. But the shape of the event is what I want to discuss, because it is a pattern rather than an isolated incident.

## What SB 253 asks for

The law requires US business entities with annual revenues above one billion dollars that do business in California to disclose Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions annually, beginning in 2026, with Scope 3 reporting commencing in 2027. SB 261, the related climate risk disclosure law, has been subject to litigation and its position has been less settled.

A three-month deferral of a first-year deadline is not a retreat from the policy. It is an administrative adjustment to a regime that had a compressed implementation timetable.

## Why first deadlines slip

Having watched several reporting regimes start, the reasons tend to be the same ones.

The regulator discovers that guidance which seemed clear in drafting is ambiguous in application, and that a large number of filers are asking the same question. Answering it properly takes longer than the runway allows.

Filers discover that data they assumed was available is held in systems that were never designed to produce it in the required shape. This is almost always the real constraint, and it is invisible until someone tries.

The submission infrastructure itself needs longer than expected. This is unglamorous and it is frequently the actual cause.

None of that indicates the requirement is going away. It indicates a first cycle behaving like a first cycle.

> A deferred deadline moves the date the work is due. It does not reduce the work, and it rarely arrives twice.

## The trap in treating it as relief

The reasonable response to three extra months is to use them. The common response is to reallocate the capacity elsewhere and return to the problem at the new deadline, having gained nothing.

I understand the pull. Reporting functions are chronically under-resourced and there is always something urgent. But the specific risk with a deferral is that it converts a known deadline into a vague one. Teams stop treating the date as fixed, because it moved once, and the internal urgency does not come back until very late.

There is also a planning asymmetry worth noting. If you prepare for the original date and the deferral holds, you are early. If you prepare for the deferred date and something goes differently than expected, you are late with no recovery time. The costs of those two errors are not symmetric.

## What the extra time is actually good for

If the deferral holds, the highest-value use of three months is not producing the submission faster. It is fixing the things that will otherwise recur every year.

**Close the completeness gaps.** Which entities, sites, or periods are missing, and why. Completeness is the [hardest thing to demonstrate](/blog/the-95-percent-rule/) and the easiest to defer indefinitely.

**Document the method while you remember it.** The emission factors chosen, the boundary decisions, the treatment of acquisitions. Written down now, this is a half-day. Reconstructed in year three, it is considerably worse.

**Build the reconciliation, not just the number.** A figure you can produce once is a submission. A figure you can reproduce, explain, and compare against the prior period is a process. Only the second one gets cheaper over time.

## The pattern across regimes

California is not unusual here. Implementation timetables for new sustainability reporting regimes have been adjusted in multiple jurisdictions, including through the EU's own Omnibus revisions, which raised CSRD thresholds and pushed first application to financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.

Reading that pattern as evidence that the direction is reversing seems to me a misreading. The consistent story across these adjustments is that the requirements arrive, more slowly than announced, in a slightly narrower form than originally drafted, and with the underlying expectation intact.

Planning for the arrival rather than the delay has been the better bet fairly consistently. I would expect that to hold.
