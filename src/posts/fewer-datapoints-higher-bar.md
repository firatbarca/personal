On 3 July 2026, the European Commission adopted the delegated act revising the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, alongside a separate voluntary standard for undertakings outside CSRD scope.

The reduction is substantial. Mandatory datapoints are cut by more than 60% and total datapoints by more than 70% against the first-generation standards, with per-company reporting costs expected to fall by more than 30%. Both acts now go to Parliament and Council for a two-month scrutiny period, which can be extended by a further two months, before entering into force. Companies in CSRD scope apply the revised standards for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.

Most of the commentary has focused on burden reduction, which is understandable. I want to look at a second-order effect that I think matters more for anyone producing the report.

## Shorter documents get read differently

A disclosure containing many hundreds of datapoints is, functionally, unreadable. No investor, auditor, or journalist interrogates every line. Attention distributes thinly, and individual figures are shielded by the sheer volume around them.

Remove most of those datapoints and the arithmetic of attention changes. What remains is closer to a document a person can actually work through, and the ones that survived the cut are, by definition, the ones the standard-setter considered most important.

> Volume was a form of protection. A shorter report gives every remaining number somewhere to be looked at closely.

This is not an argument against simplification. I think the first generation of ESRS asked for more than most organisations could produce well, and producing a large volume of unreliable information at high cost is a poor outcome for everybody. Cutting back to a defensible core is the right instinct.

But it does mean the work does not shrink proportionally with the datapoint count. If your reporting effort was 100 units across 1,000 datapoints, it does not become 40 units across 400. The remaining disclosures carry more weight, face more scrutiny, and need the evidence behind them to be correspondingly stronger.

## What this means for the reporting function

Three consequences seem likely.

**Depth replaces breadth as the constraint.** The previous cycle's challenge was coverage: getting something reasonable into every required field. The next one is defensibility: being able to substantiate a smaller number of disclosures to a higher standard. Those need different capabilities, and teams built for the first are not automatically good at the second.

**The assurance conversation gets more focused.** CSRD continues to require limited assurance from the first year of application, and the option to escalate to reasonable assurance later was removed in the Omnibus revisions. EU-level limited assurance standards are due to be adopted by July 2027. An assurance provider working through a shorter disclosure set will go deeper on each item, because there is room to.

**Datapoints that survived are the ones to invest in now.** The gap between the old and new standard is public. Working out which of your existing disclosures remain mandatory, and concentrating validation and documentation effort there, is a straightforward planning exercise that is worth doing before the FY2027 cycle rather than during it.

## A note on the voluntary standard

The voluntary standard adopted alongside the revised ESRS is easy to overlook and I suspect it will matter more than its status suggests.

Companies below the CSRD thresholds still receive data requests from customers, lenders, and insurers. Before, those requests arrived without a common reference point, which meant every requester asked in their own format and every supplier answered differently. A voluntary standard gives that exchange a shared vocabulary.

Standards that start voluntary and prove useful have a tendency to become expected. Not through legislation, but through procurement. I would not be surprised if this one follows that path.

## The part worth holding onto

I have written about [what the Omnibus did and did not change](/blog/omnibus-raised-threshold-not-ambition/), and this is the same argument at the level of the standards themselves.

Fewer things to report is a genuine improvement in a regime that had overreached. It is not the same as a lower bar for the things that remain. The organisations that read the revision as "less work" will produce shorter reports of roughly the same quality as before. The ones that read it as "the same effort, concentrated" will produce something considerably more defensible, and will find the next assurance cycle a good deal less eventful.
