This has been another summer of records — heatwaves across southern Europe, a monsoon that arrived angry rather than merely late, and yet more communities deciding, quietly and individually, that home has become untenable. My background is in political science, with a focus on climate change and human mobility, and the longer I work at the intersection of that field and corporate sustainability, the more one gap nags at me. We have grown fluent at counting tonnes of carbon. We are still almost silent on the people those tonnes displace.

Human mobility is the part of the climate story that resists a tidy metric, and so it tends to fall off the reporting page. That is a mistake — not only a moral one, but an analytical one. If we cannot see where climate change moves people, we are missing one of the clearest signals of where physical and social risk is actually landing.

## Migration is a symptom we keep mislabelling

The public conversation still reaches for the phrase "climate refugees", and it is worth being precise about why that framing misleads. Most climate-related movement is not a dramatic exodus across a border. It is internal, gradual, and economic long before it is legal: a farming household in the Sahel sending one member to the city because the last two harvests failed; a coastal family in South Asia relocating a few kilometres inland after one flood too many. The World Bank's Groundswell work has for years put the plausible scale of internal climate migration in the tens of millions by mid-century, and nothing in the intervening data has made that look pessimistic.

The point for anyone working in ESG is that these movements are rarely captured as "climate" anything. They surface as labour shortages, as pressure on urban infrastructure, as supply chains that wobble for reasons the spreadsheet files under "other". By the time a disruption is visible in a company's numbers, the human decision that caused it happened seasons earlier, somewhere upstream, to people no one was counting.

## Why this belongs in ESG, not beside it

I sometimes hear that mobility is a matter for humanitarian agencies and governments, not for corporate reporting. I understand the instinct, but I think it draws the boundary in the wrong place. Consider where a business actually meets climate mobility:

- **Supply chains.** Agricultural commodities, textiles and extractives often depend on regions where climate stress and out-migration are already reshaping the available workforce. Adaptation risk in those regions is your continuity risk.
- **Own operations.** Facilities in exposed geographies face not only physical damage from heat and flooding, but the slower erosion of the local labour pool that makes a site viable.
- **The social dimension of the "S".** Migrant and displaced workers are among the most exposed to labour abuses. A serious human-rights due-diligence process cannot pretend that climate-driven movement is someone else's file.

Framed that way, mobility is not an add-on to environmental and social reporting. It sits precisely where the "E" and the "S" meet — which is exactly the seam that most disclosure frameworks handle least well.

> We measure emissions to the kilogram and displacement to the nearest anecdote. The asymmetry is telling.

## What a credible first step looks like

I am wary of grand promises here, because the honest answer is that the data is genuinely hard and pretending otherwise helps no one. But "hard to measure" is not the same as "safe to ignore", and there is useful, unglamorous work available to anyone willing to start.

The first move is to look at your own exposure through a mobility lens rather than a purely physical one. A climate risk assessment that maps flooding and heat to assets, but stops before asking what those hazards do to the people who staff and supply those assets, has answered half the question. The second move is to treat displacement as a data-quality problem like any other: name it, note where the evidence is thin, and be explicit in disclosures about what you cannot yet see. A transparent gap is more credible than a confident silence.

None of this requires a company to solve climate migration. It requires us to stop pretending it happens outside the boundary of what we report. The organisations that will navigate the next decade well are the ones that can see the whole chain of consequence — from the tonne of carbon to the field that failed to the worker who left.

Emissions accounting taught us to make the invisible visible. Human mobility is the next thing hiding in plain sight. We already have the discipline to count what matters. We simply have not yet decided that these people count.
