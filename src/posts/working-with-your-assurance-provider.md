There is a posture I see often in first-time sustainability assurance engagements. The reporting team prepares as if for an examination: minimise what is volunteered, answer the question asked and no more, resolve nothing that was not raised.

It is an understandable instinct and I think it is expensive.

## What assurance is set up to do

CSRD requires limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application. The Omnibus revisions removed the possibility of escalating to reasonable assurance at a later date, and EU-level limited assurance standards are due to be adopted by July 2027.

Limited assurance involves less extensive procedures than reasonable assurance, and the conclusion is expressed differently. What it still requires is that the provider understands how the information was produced, tests whether the process is capable of producing it reliably, and traces specific assertions to something underneath them.

That work has a useful by-product. Someone competent, external, and without a stake in the answer spends a concentrated period examining exactly the parts of your process most likely to break. Very few reporting functions can buy that kind of review any other way.

> An assurance engagement is the only time most reporting teams get a serious external read on their controls. Treating it purely as a threat wastes it.

## Where the adversarial posture costs you

Three specific costs, all of which I have watched happen.

**Known weaknesses stay hidden until they are found.** A team that knows three countries submit late, or that a particular factor has never been re-examined, and says nothing, is not avoiding the finding. It is deferring it to the point where there is no time to respond well. Raised early, the same issue is a discussion about remediation timing. Raised in the final week, it is a problem.

**Questions get answered narrowly.** Answering exactly what was asked, when you can see what is actually being probed, produces a longer engagement with more rounds. The provider is trying to understand a process. Helping them understand it faster is in your interest.

**The learning is discarded.** The findings tell you where your process is weak. A team in defensive mode reads them as criticism to be closed out, files the responses, and repeats the cycle next year with the same weaknesses.

## What works better

**Give them the process, not just the outputs.** Walk them through how data moves from source to disclosure before they ask. It shortens everything and it surfaces the questions you would rather have early.

**Volunteer the soft spots.** Every reporting function has them. Naming yours, with what you are doing about them, changes the character of the engagement. It also signals a functioning control environment, which is itself something they are assessing.

**Keep documentation current rather than assembling it.** I have written about [audit readiness as a mindset rather than a phase](/blog/esg-data-quality/), and this is where it pays. Documentation written while the work happens is evidence. Documentation assembled afterwards is reconstruction, and experienced providers can tell the difference.

**Debrief properly.** After the engagement, work through what was raised and decide what to change. The findings are a free diagnostic of the process. Most of their value sits in the twelve months after the conclusion is signed.

## The limit of the argument

I want to be careful not to overstate this. An assurance provider is independent, and that independence is the entire point. They are not consultants, they cannot design your controls for you, and a relationship that becomes too comfortable is a problem of a different kind.

The distinction I am drawing is between adversarial and collaborative, not between independent and captured. You can be entirely straight with someone whose job is to challenge you. In fact that is generally the only way the challenge produces anything useful.

## Why it matters more now

The revised ESRS cut mandatory datapoints by more than 60%, and apply for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027. A shorter disclosure set means an assurance provider has room to go deeper on each remaining item.

The engagements ahead will be more concentrated than the ones behind. A team that has spent those cycles treating assurance as an examination will have learned to pass a test. A team that treated it as a review will have spent the same time fixing the process, which is a considerably better position to be examined from.
