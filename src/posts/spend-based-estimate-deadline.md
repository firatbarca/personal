Multiply what you paid by an industry-average factor, and you have a Scope 3 number. It takes an afternoon. It has carried a remarkable amount of corporate carbon accounting for over a decade.

That approach is now under review at the standard-setting level. On 31 March 2026, the GHG Protocol published its Scope 3 Phase 1 Progress Update, the first substantial rework of the Scope 3 Standard since it was issued in 2011. The final revised standard is targeted for late 2027, with a full public consultation draft expected before then, so nothing here is settled. But the direction of travel is legible, and it points somewhere specific.

## What the draft proposes

Two proposals matter more than the rest.

The first is a **95% minimum coverage floor**, with any exclusions capped and justified with data. Today, incomplete Scope 3 inventories are common and the gaps are often invisible in the published figure. A coverage floor turns completeness into something you either meet or explain.

The second is **disaggregation by data type**. Reported Scope 3 emissions would be separated into tiers distinguishing specific (primary) data, secondary data, and spend-based estimates. The total stops being a single number and becomes a number with its composition attached.

That second proposal is the one I keep thinking about.

## Why tiering changes the conversation

Right now, a Scope 3 figure built almost entirely from spend-based estimates and a Scope 3 figure built from supplier-specific measurement look identical on the page. Same units, same font, same position in the report. A reader cannot tell them apart, which means the organisation that invested years in supplier data collection gets no visible credit for it, and the organisation that ran a spreadsheet multiplication faces no visible cost.

Tiering removes that symmetry.

> Estimation was never the problem. Estimation that is indistinguishable from measurement is the problem.

I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this argument that overreaches. Spend-based estimation is a legitimate method. For a long tail of small, diverse suppliers it may remain the only sensible one, and pretending otherwise wastes effort that belongs elsewhere. Even financial accounting depends on estimates. The issue has never been that estimates exist. It is that nothing in the current presentation tells you which parts of a total are estimated and which are measured.

Once that composition is visible, the incentive structure changes on its own. Nobody has to mandate primary data collection. A company reporting 90% spend-based coverage next to a competitor reporting 60% will find that the difference gets noticed, priced, and eventually asked about.

## What this means if you are collecting the data

I have written before about [ESG controlling living and dying on data quality](/blog/esg-data-quality/), and this proposal sits directly in that space. A few practical implications, on the assumption the direction holds:

**Know your current mix now.** Most organisations cannot say what proportion of their Scope 3 total comes from primary data. If tiering arrives, that becomes a number you report rather than a number you estimate under pressure. Establishing the baseline early is much easier than reconstructing it later.

**Coverage is a data governance problem, not a procurement one.** A 95% floor is a completeness question, and completeness is the thing reporting functions are usually weakest at demonstrating. Knowing which categories are missing, and why, is the foundation.

**Purchased goods and services is the pressure point.** It is the largest Scope 3 category for most sectors and among the least reported with primary data. Any tightening will be felt there first.

## The longer arc

There is a pattern in how reporting standards mature, and it is fairly consistent. First the requirement is that you produce a number. Then the requirement is that you produce a number with a stated method. Then the requirement is that you show which parts of the number rest on measurement and which rest on assumption.

Financial reporting walked this path over decades. Sustainability reporting is walking it considerably faster, which is uncomfortable but not unreasonable given what the numbers are now used for.

The draft revision is not final, and the consultation will change parts of it. But the underlying idea, that a figure should carry evidence of its own quality, is not the sort of idea that gets reversed. It is worth preparing for the version of this that arrives, rather than the version you would prefer.
