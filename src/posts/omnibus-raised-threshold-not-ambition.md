The Omnibus package is no longer a proposal to track. It is law, and the implementation clock is running.

The Council signed off on the simplification of sustainability reporting and due diligence requirements on 24 February 2026. The directive was published in the Official Journal on 26 February and entered into force on 18 March 2026. Member States have twelve months to transpose the CSRD provisions, with first application required for financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.

The headline changes are real. Scope thresholds rose to more than 1,000 employees and more than 450 million euro turnover, which removes a substantial number of companies from direct scope. On 3 July 2026 the European Commission adopted the delegated act revising the ESRS, cutting mandatory datapoints by more than 60% and total datapoints by more than 70%, with per-company reporting costs expected to fall by over 30%. Both acts now go to Parliament and Council for a two-month scrutiny period, extendable by a further two months, before entering into force.

That is a genuine reduction in burden. It is worth being clear about what it is not.

## Scope is not the same as relevance

A company that drops out of direct CSRD scope has not stopped emitting, and has not stopped being part of somebody else's value chain. What changes is who requires the disclosure, not whether the underlying information has value.

This matters most for companies in the middle. If you sit below the new thresholds but supply organisations that sit above them, the request for your data does not disappear with your reporting obligation. It arrives through procurement instead of regulation, which is often less structured and rarely less demanding.

> Simplification changed who has to report. It did not change who has to know.

The Omnibus was explicit about limiting the trickle-down of obligations onto smaller companies, and that intent deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed. A voluntary standard for undertakings outside CSRD scope was adopted alongside the revised ESRS on the same day, which gives smaller organisations a proportionate reference point rather than a scaled-down version of a regime designed for large groups.

But a voluntary standard is still a standard. Companies that use it will be able to answer questions their competitors cannot.

## What did not get simpler

Two things survived the simplification largely intact, and both sit in the part of the work I spend my time on.

**Assurance.** CSRD continues to require limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application. What was removed was the option to escalate to reasonable assurance at a later date. EU-level limited assurance standards are due to be adopted by July 2027.

The word "limited" does a lot of work in conversations about this, and not always accurately. Limited assurance is a lower bar than reasonable assurance, but it is not an absent one. An assurance provider still asks where a figure came from, still tests whether the process behind it is capable of producing it reliably, and still forms a conclusion they put their name to.

**Provenance.** Fewer datapoints means fewer things to evidence. It does not mean the remaining things need less evidence. If anything, a shorter disclosure concentrates attention. When a report contains four hundred datapoints, no reader interrogates any single one closely. When it contains a fraction of that, each one carries more weight.

## The strategic read

I understand why the simplification happened, and I think parts of it were overdue. The first generation of ESRS asked for a volume of information that few organisations could produce well, and producing something badly at great expense serves nobody. Cutting datapoints by 60% or more is not a retreat from ambition if what disappears was never being reported reliably.

The risk is a different one. Reduced scope creates a natural temptation to stand down capability that took years to build: the validation routines, the reconciliation logic, the ownership model that puts a name against every data point. That capability was expensive precisely because it is hard, and it will be considerably more expensive to rebuild than to maintain.

The regulatory floor moved. The reasons for wanting defensible sustainability data, which include financing terms, procurement qualification, and the plain operational value of knowing where your energy and emissions actually sit, did not move with it.

Companies that treat the Omnibus as permission to stop will be fine for a few years. Companies that treat it as room to do the remaining work properly will be in a better position when the questions come from somewhere other than a regulator.
