I have argued that [reaching net zero is a milestone rather than a finish line](/blog/beyond-net-zero-carbon-removal/), and that removal has to become part of the picture rather than an afterthought to reduction.

Here is the part I did not spend enough time on. A removal claim is a data claim, and it has all the same weaknesses as the emissions data the profession has spent years learning to discipline. It may have more.

## Why removal is harder to evidence than emission

An emissions figure describes something that happened. Fuel was burned, electricity was drawn, freight moved. The event is in the past and it left traces in invoices, meters, and shipping records. Verification is difficult, but there is something to verify against.

A removal claim frequently describes something that will continue happening. A tonne removed and stored is only genuinely removed if it stays stored, and the storage period that matters is measured in decades or centuries. The evidence for the claim therefore includes evidence about the future, which is a different sort of assertion.

That produces three problems the emissions side does not have in the same form.

**Duration.** How long the storage is expected to last, and what happens to the claim if it does not.

**Additionality.** Whether the removal would have occurred anyway, which requires reasoning about a counterfactual rather than observing an event.

**Ownership.** Who is entitled to count the tonne, and whether it has been counted by anyone else. Emissions have a fairly clear allocation logic. Removal credits move through markets, and a tonne that changes hands can be claimed more than once if the registry discipline is weak.

> A removal claim asks you to trust an event, a projection, and a chain of custody at the same time.

## The familiar failure, rehearsed again

What concerns me is how closely the current state of removal claims resembles where emissions reporting was some years ago.

Numbers arrive with a certificate attached. The certificate references a methodology. The methodology is complex and the buyer has not read it. Everyone in the chain assumes somebody upstream did the verification. The claim ends up in a sustainability statement as a clean figure with no visible provenance, and it looks exactly as solid as a figure that was checked properly.

That is the [guesswork problem](/blog/from-guesswork-to-audit-trails/) in a new market. Not because anyone is being dishonest, but because the trail was never built and nobody noticed it was missing while the stakes were low.

The stakes are not going to stay low. As removal features in transition plans that feed financing decisions, an unverifiable removal claim becomes a financial exposure rather than a reputational one.

## What a defensible removal claim carries

The requirements are not exotic. They are the same ones we apply to any reported figure, adapted.

- **The source.** Which project, which registry, which serial numbers. Specific enough to look up.
- **The method.** Which protocol was applied, in which version, and what it requires.
- **The retirement record.** Evidence that the credit was retired against your claim and cannot be claimed again.
- **The durability basis.** The expected storage period and what underwrites it.
- **The assumptions, labelled.** Where the claim rests on projection rather than measurement, stated as such.

None of that is difficult to collect at the point of purchase. Almost all of it is difficult to reconstruct two years later, which is the pattern that should be familiar to anyone who has worked a reporting cycle.

## The argument I would make internally

Removal is going to be necessary. The reductions that are technically or economically unavailable in the near term have to be addressed somehow, and pretending otherwise does not make the residual emissions disappear.

But the credibility of the whole approach depends on the claims being defensible individually. A market where good removal and weak removal are indistinguishable in a report is a market where the price signal rewards the cheaper one, and that is a bad outcome for the technologies that actually work.

The profession learned this on the emissions side the slow way. Numbers that could not be traced were eventually challenged, and the response was to build provenance from scratch under time pressure.

There is no reason to repeat that sequence. The discipline exists, it is well understood, and it can be applied to removal claims from the beginning rather than retrofitted after the first uncomfortable question.
