Two developments have been running in parallel and are rarely discussed together. Sustainability disclosures are increasingly being drafted with AI assistance. And those same disclosures remain subject to external assurance.

The Omnibus revisions kept limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application, while removing the option to escalate to reasonable assurance at a later date. EU-level limited assurance standards are due to be adopted by July 2027.

That combination creates a practical question that I do not think has been thought through carefully enough: what does an assurance provider do when part of the text in front of them was generated rather than written.

## What limited assurance actually involves

The word "limited" invites a misreading. Limited assurance is a lower level of confidence than reasonable assurance, and the procedures are less extensive. It is not an absence of examination.

An assurance provider forms a conclusion, in writing, with their name on it. To do that they need to understand how the information was produced, test whether the process is capable of producing it reliably, and follow specific assertions back to something underneath them. The conclusion is expressed negatively, along the lines of nothing having come to their attention suggesting the statement is materially misstated, but arriving at that position still requires work.

Every one of those procedures assumes an answerable question: where did this come from.

## Where generated text creates a gap

A human-drafted disclosure has an implicit chain behind it. Somebody read a dataset, formed a view, and wrote a sentence. If challenged, they can usually reconstruct their reasoning, point at the source, and explain why they characterised it the way they did.

Generated text can arrive without that chain. A well-constructed paragraph describing a company's transition plan may be entirely accurate, entirely consistent with the underlying data, and still have nobody who can say how it came to make the specific claims it makes. The output is fluent, which makes the absence easy to miss.

> Fluency is not evidence. A sentence that reads as though someone verified it is not the same as a sentence someone verified.

This is the same boundary I described in [AI in ESG reporting as assistant rather than author](/blog/ai-in-esg-reporting-assistant-not-author/), viewed from the assurance side rather than the drafting side. The tool can touch the inputs. A person has to own the output, and owning it means being able to account for it under questioning.

## A workable division of labour

None of this argues for keeping AI out of the reporting process. It argues for being deliberate about which parts of the work it touches.

Uses that sit comfortably with assurance:

- **Extraction and consolidation**, where the output is checkable against the source document it came from
- **Consistency checking**, such as finding places where a figure in the narrative disagrees with the same figure in a table
- **First-draft structure**, where a human then writes the substantive claims
- **Translation and readability**, applied to text whose content is already settled

Uses that create difficulty:

- **Generating substantive assertions** about performance, targets, or plans without a named person verifying each one
- **Producing explanations for variances** that were not independently investigated
- **Filling gaps** where the underlying data is incomplete, since fluent text over a data gap is precisely what completeness testing is designed to catch

## The documentation habit that solves most of it

The practical fix is unremarkable and it is the same fix as always: record how the text was produced, at the time it was produced.

If a section was AI-assisted, note it internally, note who reviewed it, and note what they checked against. Not for disclosure, but so that in eight months, when someone asks how a specific sentence came to be in the statement, the answer exists somewhere other than in a person's memory.

That habit is not new and it is not about AI. It is the same discipline that turned ESG reporting from a set of plausible numbers into a set of defensible ones. Tooling changed how the text gets made. It has not changed what an assurance provider is entitled to ask about it, and the standards arriving by July 2027 are unlikely to relax on that point.

The organisations that will handle this well are the ones treating an AI-drafted paragraph the way they would treat a figure submitted by a country office: useful, plausible, and not going into the report until somebody has checked it and put their name against the check.
