# Understanding ESRS: Double Materiality, Disclosures and Reporting Evidence

The European Sustainability Reporting Standards—**ESRS**—are the detailed standards used to prepare a sustainability statement under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). They translate a legal duty to report into a structured process: identify material sustainability matters, explain how they are governed and managed, and support the resulting metrics and narratives with evidence.

ESRS are sometimes described as a long checklist. That description misses their organising principle. The starting point is not the datapoint; it is **double materiality**.

There is also a version issue to handle carefully. The first ESRS were adopted in 2023. On 3 July 2026, the European Commission adopted a substantially revised set, reducing mandatory datapoints by more than 60% and total datapoints by more than 70%. The revised standards were published in the Official Journal on 21 September 2026 as [Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1563](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32026R1563). As at 19 October 2026, the regulation has been published but has not yet entered into force. It enters into force on 10 November 2026 and applies to financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027.

Transitional provisions also allow undertakings within the relevant reporting scope to use the revised standards for financial years beginning in 2026 once the regulation enters into force. Alternatively, they may continue applying the previous standards, with the option to use certain specified reliefs introduced by the revision. Undertakings must identify the version applied in their sustainability statement. This distinction matters when comparing reports prepared under different versions of ESRS.

## The terminology that makes ESRS readable

**Disclosure requirement (DR).** A defined reporting requirement specifying information to be disclosed. A disclosure requirement can contain several individual datapoints.

**Datapoint.** A specific quantitative or narrative item within a disclosure requirement: for example, a figure, policy description, target, methodology or explanation. Disclosure requirements and datapoints are subject to the applicable materiality provisions.

**Impact, risk and opportunity (IRO).** An *impact* is an actual or potential, positive or negative effect connected with the company on people or the environment. A *risk* or *opportunity* is a sustainability-related matter that affects, or could reasonably be expected to affect, the company's financial prospects. ESRS require companies to assess both perspectives.

**Impact materiality.** A topic is material from an impact perspective when it relates to significant actual or potential, positive or negative impacts on people or the environment. For an actual negative impact, severity is assessed through scale, scope and irremediable character. For a potential negative impact, likelihood is considered alongside severity; for potential negative human-rights impacts, severity takes precedence over likelihood. Actual positive impacts are assessed through scale and scope, while potential positive impacts also consider likelihood. Positive and negative impacts are assessed separately rather than netted against each other.

**Financial materiality.** A topic is financially material when related sustainability risks or opportunities have, or could reasonably be expected to have, material effects on the company's development, financial position, financial performance, cash flows, access to finance or cost of capital over the short, medium or long term. Financial materiality covers existing as well as reasonably anticipated effects.

**Double materiality.** The two perspectives are assessed together, but they are not a two-part test that both must be passed. A matter is material if it is material from the impact perspective, the financial perspective, or both.

**Information materiality.** Identifying a material topic does not automatically make every associated datapoint material. The revised ESRS distinguish between identifying topics connected with material IROs and determining which information about those topics needs to be reported. Information is material when omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence relevant users' decisions.

## How the standards are organised

ESRS 1 explains the general requirements, including materiality, reporting boundaries, value-chain information and preparation principles. ESRS 2 contains general disclosures about governance, strategy and the management of impacts, risks and opportunities. Topical standards then address environmental, social and governance matters.

The revised 2026 standards retain four recurring reporting areas:

- **Governance**—who oversees sustainability matters and how.
- **Strategy**—how material IROs relate to the business model, strategy and financial effects.
- **Management of IROs**—the policies and actions used to manage material impacts, risks and opportunities.
- **Metrics and targets**—how performance and progress are measured.

This architecture matters because a complete disclosure is rarely just a number. An emissions figure without a boundary, methodology, explanation of material assumptions and relevant context is difficult to interpret and difficult to assure. Clear internal ownership and supporting documentation make it more reliable.

The revised standards also make the distinction between **topic materiality and information materiality** particularly important. Companies first identify topics associated with material IROs and then determine the information to disclose about those topics.

ESRS 2 general disclosures remain part of this reporting framework, while the applicable topical requirements are determined by the material IROs and the information materiality assessment. The general disclosure requirements for policies, actions, metrics and targets are applied where relevant to material topics. Entity-specific information must be added where necessary for fair presentation.

## A practical double-materiality process

A defensible assessment can be organised into six stages. These stages provide a practical workflow rather than a single mandatory sequence prescribed by ESRS.

First, understand the business model, activities, products, geographies, stakeholders and business relationships. Second, identify where significant impacts, risks or opportunities are likely to arise. Third, use evidence from affected stakeholders, existing due diligence processes and appropriate experts where their perspectives are relevant. Fourth, assess impacts and financial effects using documented criteria and thresholds, including qualitative judgements where appropriate. Fifth, validate the conclusions through the company's governance process. Finally, map material topics to applicable disclosure requirements and any necessary entity-specific information.

The revised standards explicitly support a proportionate, **top-down approach**. A company may reach a materiality conclusion using its strategy, business model, sectors, geographies and value-chain characteristics where the conclusion is sufficiently evident. Where it is not evident, more specific assessment is necessary. The standards also permit a bottom-up approach or a combination of approaches.

The revised text does not require an exhaustive search across every possible IRO. Companies may use reasonable and supportable information available without undue cost or effort and focus on areas where material impacts, risks or opportunities are likely to arise.

That relief is not permission to ignore difficult parts of the value chain. It allows the assessment to concentrate on relevant areas while retaining sufficient evidence for the conclusions reached.

Stakeholder engagement should not be treated as a popularity survey. Existing engagement undertaken through sustainability due diligence can provide evidence without requiring a separate consultation exercise solely for the materiality assessment. The purpose is to understand impacts and supporting evidence, not to let respondents vote on the final disclosure list.

**Entity-specific information** is information needed for fair presentation when the prescribed ESRS disclosures do not adequately describe a material company-specific matter. Standards cannot anticipate every business model, so compliance can require more than mechanically completing listed datapoints.

## Value-chain information and estimates

ESRS extend beyond own operations when necessary to understand material IROs connected with a company through direct or indirect business relationships. The **upstream value chain** generally covers inputs and suppliers; the **downstream value chain** generally covers distribution, use and end-of-life relationships.

The standards do not require primary data from every actor. The revised text permits the use of estimates where appropriate, taking account of practicability and reliability. Estimates may draw on sector averages, sample analyses, market information, spend-based data and other proxies.

An estimate is not automatically weak evidence. An undocumented estimate is.

A well-supported estimate records:

- The population and reporting boundary it represents.
- The source, relevance and age of the input data.
- The calculation method and significant assumptions.
- Known uncertainty, limitations and data gaps.
- The approach to improving data coverage or quality where improvement is material.

The revised standards also contain specific reliefs where reliable data can be obtained only for part of the reporting boundary. These reliefs are subject to conditions and disclosure requirements, and do not apply identically to every metric. In particular, the partial-reporting-scope relief in ESRS 1 paragraph 91 excludes the gross Scope 1, 2 and 3 greenhouse-gas emissions metrics under ESRS E1-8.

Where partial reporting is permitted, companies must explain the limitation and the actions taken to improve coverage and quality in subsequent reporting periods. The standards expect the availability of relevant information to improve over time.

An important distinction remains: using an estimate is a measurement choice, while excluding part of the reporting boundary is a scope limitation. The two should not be confused.

## What not to do

Three errors recur in first reporting cycles.

The first is treating every ESRS datapoint as mandatory regardless of materiality. Under the revised approach, immaterial information generally should not be reported because it can obscure what matters, subject to the provisions permitting supplementary information. Companies must still apply ESRS 2, the relevant topical requirements and any required disclosures associated with reporting reliefs.

The second is treating stakeholder engagement as a popularity survey. The purpose is to understand impacts and evidence, not to let respondents determine materiality through voting or preference rankings.

The third is using a polished materiality matrix as a substitute for an audit trail. A matrix may communicate conclusions, but it does not explain how evidence was gathered, how thresholds were applied, why judgements were reached or how reported information was checked.

The real output of the assessment is not the matrix. It is a controlled chain from evidence, to IRO, to materiality judgement, to disclosure requirement, to reported figure or narrative.

That chain matters for governance, consistency, repeatability and assurance. It also makes the next reporting cycle easier because changes in data, assumptions and materiality conclusions can be traced rather than reconstructed.

## Official sources and verification

**Last verified: 19 October 2026.**

The revised ESRS were published in the Official Journal on 21 September 2026. They enter into force on 10 November 2026 and apply to financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027, subject to the transitional options for financial years beginning in 2026.

The following official sources support the legislative timeline and technical explanations:

- [Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/1563 — published regulation and revised ESRS](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32026R1563). The primary legal source, including the revised standards, transitional provisions and application dates.
- [Official Journal PDF of Regulation (EU) 2026/1563](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202601563). The published regulation in PDF form.
- [Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772 — original ESRS](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023R2772). The original framework, relevant when comparing reporting requirements and transitional options.
- [European Commission announcement of revised ESRS, 3 July 2026](https://finance.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-adopts-revised-sustainability-reporting-standards-2026-07-03_en). Confirms the adoption and reported datapoint reductions.
- [European Commission adopted annex containing revised ESRS](https://ec.europa.eu/finance/docs/level-2-measures/csrd-delegated-act-2026-5010-annex_en.pdf). Useful for reviewing the structure and provisions of the revised standards. The Official Journal version remains the definitive published legal text.

For technical verification, the most relevant provisions of revised ESRS 1 are paragraphs 11, 17, 23–33, 35–50, 62–65, 88, 90–95 and the associated Application Requirements. Article 2 and Article 3 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1563 govern the transitional options, entry into force and application dates.

Because the applicable reporting framework depends on the financial year and the undertaking's reporting status, companies should confirm which version of ESRS applies before preparing or publishing a sustainability statement.
