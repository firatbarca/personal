The European Sustainability Reporting Standards—**ESRS**—are the detailed standards used to prepare a sustainability statement under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD). They translate a legal duty to report into a structured process: identify material sustainability matters, explain how they are governed and managed, and support the resulting metrics with evidence.

ESRS are sometimes described as a long checklist. That description misses their organising principle. The starting point is not the datapoint; it is **double materiality**.

There is also a version issue to handle carefully. The first ESRS were adopted in 2023. The Commission adopted a substantially revised set on 3 July 2026, reducing mandatory datapoints by more than 60% and total datapoints by more than 70%. As at 1 September 2026, the revised delegated act was still described by the Commission as not in force pending publication in the Official Journal. Its adopted text provides for application from financial year 2027 and permits early use for financial year 2026 once effective.

## The terminology that makes ESRS readable

**Disclosure requirement (DR).** A defined reporting objective or block of information required by a standard. A disclosure requirement can contain several individual datapoints.

**Datapoint.** A specific quantitative or narrative item within a disclosure requirement: for example, a figure, policy description, target, methodology or explanation.

**Impact, risk and opportunity (IRO).** An *impact* is an effect the company has or could have on people or the environment. A *risk* or *opportunity* is a sustainability matter that could affect the company’s prospects. ESRS require companies to assess both perspectives.

**Impact materiality.** A topic is material when it relates to significant actual or potential, positive or negative impacts on people or the environment. For an actual negative impact, severity is assessed through scale, scope and irremediable character. For a potential negative impact, likelihood is considered alongside severity; for potential human-rights impacts, severity takes precedence over likelihood.

**Financial materiality.** A topic is material when it could reasonably be expected to affect financial performance, financial position, cash flows, access to finance or cost of capital over the short, medium or long term.

**Double materiality.** The two perspectives are assessed together, but they are not a two-part test that both must be passed. A matter is material if it is material from the impact perspective, the financial perspective, or both.

## How the standards are organised

ESRS 1 explains the general requirements, including materiality, reporting boundaries, value-chain information and preparation principles. ESRS 2 contains general disclosures about governance, strategy and the process for managing impacts, risks and opportunities. Topical standards then address environmental, social and governance matters.

The revised 2026 text retains four recurring reporting areas:

- **governance**—who oversees the matter and how;
- **strategy**—how it affects the business model, strategy and financial outlook;
- **management of IROs**—the policies and actions used to address it; and
- **metrics and targets**—how performance and progress are measured.

This architecture matters because a complete disclosure is rarely just a number. An emissions figure without a boundary, methodology, responsible owner and explanation of movement is difficult to interpret and difficult to assure.

## A practical double-materiality process

A defensible assessment normally moves through six stages.

First, understand the business model, activities, products, geographies and business relationships. Second, identify where significant impacts, risks or opportunities are likely to arise. Third, engage affected stakeholders and appropriate experts where their perspectives are needed. Fourth, assess impacts and financial effects using documented criteria and thresholds. Fifth, validate and approve the conclusions through the company’s governance process. Finally, map each material topic to the applicable disclosure requirements and entity-specific information.

The revised text explicitly supports a proportionate, top-down approach. It says a company is not required to perform an exhaustive search across every possible IRO and may use reasonable and supportable information available without undue cost or effort. That relief is not permission to ignore difficult parts of the value chain. It allows the work to focus on places where material matters are reasonably likely.

**Entity-specific information** is information needed for fair presentation when the prescribed ESRS disclosures do not fully describe a material company-specific matter. Standards cannot anticipate every business model, so compliance can require more than mechanically completing the listed datapoints.

## Value-chain information and estimates

ESRS extend beyond own operations when that is necessary to understand material IROs connected through direct or indirect business relationships. The **upstream value chain** generally covers inputs and suppliers; the **downstream value chain** generally covers distribution, use and end-of-life relationships.

The standards do not require primary data from every actor. The revised text permits estimates based on sector averages, sample analyses, market information, spend-based data and other proxies when direct data are not practicable or reliable enough. An estimate is not automatically weak evidence. An undocumented estimate is.

A good estimate records:

- the population and boundary it represents;
- the source and age of the input data;
- the calculation method and assumptions;
- known uncertainty or limitations; and
- the plan for improving the method where improvement is material.

## What not to do

Three errors recur in first reporting cycles.

The first is treating every ESRS datapoint as mandatory regardless of materiality. Under the revised approach, immaterial information generally should not be reported because it can obscure what matters. The second is treating stakeholder engagement as a popularity survey; the purpose is to understand impacts and evidence, not to let respondents vote on the final disclosure list. The third is using a polished materiality matrix as a substitute for an audit trail.

The real output of the assessment is not the matrix. It is a controlled chain from evidence, to IRO, to materiality judgement, to disclosure, to reported figure or narrative.
