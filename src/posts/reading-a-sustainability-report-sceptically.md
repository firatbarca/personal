Most people who read a sustainability report are not in a position to audit it. Investors, procurement teams, journalists, and job candidates are looking at a document produced by people with far more information than they have, and the document is designed to be persuasive.

You can still tell a good deal from structure alone. The signals below are ones I use, and none of them require checking a calculation.

## Signals that the underlying process is sound

**Estimates are labelled as estimates.** A report distinguishing measured from estimated figures is telling you it knows the difference and can identify which is which. That capability is harder to build than it sounds, and organisations that have it tend to have the rest.

**Coverage is stated, not implied.** "Covers 94% of operations by revenue, excluding three recently acquired sites" is a stronger disclosure than a figure presented without qualification. The second one is not more complete. It is less explicit about being incomplete.

**Prior-year figures have been restated where needed, with a bridge.** A restatement with a clear explanation of cause and a reconciliation between old and new is [evidence of a working control environment](/blog/how-to-restate-a-sustainability-figure/), not a red flag.

**Changes in the trend are decomposed.** If emissions fell and the organisation also divested something, a report that separates organic change from portfolio change is being straight with you.

**Method changes are disclosed with their effect quantified.** Not just "we updated our methodology" but what the update did to the number.

## Signals worth a second look

None of these prove anything is wrong. They indicate places where the report is doing less work than it appears to.

**A perfectly smooth multi-year trend.** Real operations are lumpy. Weather varies, production varies, sites open and close. A curve with no irregularities has usually been smoothed somewhere, whether by estimation methodology or by a boundary that moves conveniently.

**Complete Scope 3 coverage achieved quickly across a diverse supplier base.** Primary supplier data is slow and expensive to collect. Comprehensive coverage arriving fast generally means spend-based estimation is doing heavy lifting, which is legitimate but should be visible.

**Targets restated more often than results.** Frequent rebaselining of a target, particularly in the direction of achievability, tells you more about the reporting culture than the underlying performance does.

**A materiality assessment with no exclusions explained.** Every assessment excludes topics. A report that presents only what was included has not shown you the judgment that shaped the whole document.

**Narrative density around a weak number.** Sections where the prose expands considerably while the quantitative disclosure stays thin are usually not accidental.

> The most informative parts of a sustainability report are the places where it tells you what it does not know.

## Two structural questions worth asking

**What is assured, and to what level?** CSRD requires limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application, and the option to escalate to reasonable assurance later was removed in the Omnibus revisions, with EU-level limited assurance standards due by July 2027. Limited assurance is a genuine examination, but it is less extensive than reasonable assurance, and the scope of what was assured is often narrower than readers assume. The assurance statement itself will say.

**How much of the emissions figure rests on primary data?** Few reports answer this today. The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 revision, published as a progress update in March 2026, is consulting on separating reported emissions into tiers distinguishing specific, secondary, and spend-based data. If that direction holds, this becomes answerable. Until then, a report that volunteers the split is telling you something about itself.

## The disposition I would recommend

Scepticism here is not the same as cynicism. The overwhelming majority of sustainability reports are produced in good faith by people working with imperfect data and real constraints, and treating every disclosure as spin is both unfair and analytically useless.

What is useful is reading for evidence of process rather than for the quality of the numbers. You cannot verify a figure from the outside. You can assess whether the organisation appears to know where its figures came from, and organisations that demonstrate that are reliably better bets than organisations that simply present clean results.

The question is the same one I keep returning to, just asked from the other side of the page: not what the number is, but whether they can tell you how they know it.
