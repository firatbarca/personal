Reporting teams spend most of their effort on accuracy. Is this figure correct, is the factor right, does the calculation hold up. Far less effort goes to a question that is harder to answer and often matters more: is anything missing.

The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 Phase 1 Progress Update, published on 31 March 2026, includes a proposal that puts that second question in the foreground. A 95% minimum coverage floor, with exclusions capped and justified with data. The revision is still in progress, with a full public consultation expected and the final standard targeted for late 2027, so the number itself may move. The concept is what interests me.

## Why completeness is the harder problem

Accuracy errors are visible in the data. A figure that is wrong by an order of magnitude usually announces itself in a variance check. A factor applied incorrectly shows up when the period comparison breaks.

Completeness errors are invisible in the data, because the data is not there. Nothing in a spreadsheet flags the site that never submitted, the category that was excluded during scoping three years ago, or the acquisition that was never added to the reporting boundary. The total looks exactly as complete as a genuinely complete total would look.

> An inaccurate number gets challenged. A missing number gets no challenge at all, because nobody sees an absence.

This is why a coverage floor is a more demanding requirement than it first appears. It does not ask you to improve your numbers. It asks you to enumerate what you do not have, which requires knowing the shape of the whole before you can describe the gap.

## What it takes to answer

To state coverage credibly, an organisation needs three things that many do not currently maintain.

**A defined universe.** Coverage is a ratio, and a ratio needs a denominator. That means a maintained list of entities, sites, categories, and spend that should be in scope, kept current as the organisation acquires, divests, and restructures. This list is separate from the data itself and is usually the thing that does not exist.

**A reason for each exclusion.** Not a note that something is out of scope, but the basis: immaterial by a stated test, data unavailable with a described attempt to obtain it, or excluded by a methodological choice with a rationale. The proposal's language about justifying exclusions with data points directly at this.

**A record of when the assessment was made.** Materiality judgments age. A category that was genuinely immaterial when assessed in 2022 may not be now, and an inventory that carries forward old exclusions without review will drift out of accuracy without anyone touching a number.

## The awkward part

There is an uncomfortable implication in coverage floors that is worth naming honestly.

Organisations with good completeness discipline will report gaps. Organisations with poor discipline may report none, because they have not looked hard enough to find any. In the short term, the diligent organisation can look worse.

That asymmetry is not a reason to avoid the requirement, but it is a reason to expect a difficult first cycle, and to prepare the internal conversation before the number lands on someone's desk. A disclosed gap with a plan attached is a sign of a functioning process. It reads that way to an assurance provider, and it should read that way internally too.

I have seen versions of this dynamic in [ESG controlling work more generally](/blog/esg-data-quality/). The teams that look most comfortable in an audit are usually the ones that arrived already knowing their weaknesses, because they had built the checks that surface them.

## What to do before it arrives

The proposal is not final and the detail will change. But the underlying capability, being able to state what proportion of your inventory rests on actual data, is useful regardless of what the standard eventually says.

A reasonable starting point is to construct the denominator for one Scope 3 category, ideally purchased goods and services, since it is the largest for most sectors and among the least completely reported. Work out what should be in it, compare that against what is in it, and write down the difference with reasons.

The first attempt is usually uncomfortable. It is also considerably cheaper to be uncomfortable now, on your own timetable, than in the first cycle where someone external is asking.
