The Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (**SFDR**) is the EU transparency framework for sustainability information in financial services. It requires financial market participants and financial advisers to explain how sustainability is considered at entity and product level.

It was designed as a disclosure regime, not a rating system. Nevertheless, the market came to use its best-known product provisions—Articles 6, 8 and 9—as informal labels. That is one reason the Commission proposed a major revision in November 2025.

As at 1 September 2026, the revision remained a legislative proposal. The proposed categories described later in this article were not yet the operative law. That status distinction is essential.

## The terminology behind the current framework

**Financial market participant (FMP).** A regulated product manufacturer or asset owner within the SFDR definitions, such as certain investment firms, fund managers, pension-product providers or insurers offering insurance-based investment products.

**Financial adviser.** A regulated firm or intermediary providing specified investment or insurance advice.

**Sustainability risk.** An environmental, social or governance event or condition that, if it occurs, could cause an actual or potential material negative effect on an investment’s value.

**Sustainability factors.** Environmental, social and employee matters, respect for human rights, anti-corruption and anti-bribery matters.

**Sustainable investment.** Under the current SFDR, an investment in an economic activity contributing to an environmental or social objective, provided it does no significant harm to those objectives and the investee company follows good-governance practices.

**Principal adverse impacts (PAI).** The most significant negative effects that investment decisions may have on sustainability factors. The detailed rules use indicators covering matters such as greenhouse-gas emissions, biodiversity, water, waste and social issues.

**Pre-contractual, website and periodic disclosures.** Information provided before investment, maintained online and reported after the relevant period. The three locations perform different functions and should be consistent.

## What Articles 6, 8 and 9 mean today

Article 6 is the baseline product provision. It requires disclosure of how sustainability risks are integrated into investment decisions and assessment of their likely effect on returns, or a clear explanation where they are considered not relevant.

Article 8 applies where a product promotes environmental or social characteristics, or a combination of them, provided investee companies follow good-governance practices. Article 8 does not by itself prescribe a single minimum environmental ambition.

Article 9 applies where a product has sustainable investment as its objective, with specific provisions also covering products with a carbon-emissions-reduction objective.

These articles describe disclosure obligations triggered by product features. They do not form a simple bronze-silver-gold quality scale. An Article 8 product is not necessarily “less good” than an Article 9 product, and classification alone says little about fees, risk, diversification, performance or suitability for a particular investor.

The **do no significant harm** test in the SFDR definition of sustainable investment should also not be confused with the similarly named test in the EU Taxonomy. They are connected within the sustainable-finance framework but have different legal formulations and applications.

## What an investor should actually read

The article number is only a starting point. Useful questions include:

- What environmental or social characteristics or objectives are binding?
- How are they measured, and against what benchmark or investment universe?
- What share of the portfolio is expected to meet each commitment?
- Which exclusions apply?
- How are PAI indicators considered?
- What data are estimated, and how are data gaps handled?
- Did the periodic report show that the product met its stated characteristics or objective?

The EU Taxonomy percentage is another specific datapoint, not an overall product score. A product can pursue a social objective for which the environmental Taxonomy provides limited classification coverage. Conversely, a Taxonomy percentage does not reveal every sustainability effect of the portfolio.

## The proposed SFDR revision

In November 2025, the Commission proposed replacing the market’s de facto use of Articles 8 and 9 with three voluntary product categories:

- **sustainable**—products contributing to environmental or social sustainability goals;
- **transition**—products financing companies, assets or projects on a credible improvement path; and
- **ESG basics**—products integrating other ESG approaches that do not meet the first two categories.

Under the Commission proposal, a categorised product would generally need at least 70% of its portfolio to support the selected strategy and would be subject to exclusions. Sustainability-related claims in product names and marketing would be reserved for categorised products. The proposal would also simplify disclosures and remove some current entity-level PAI requirements.

Those are proposed rules, not a description of what firms were legally required to do on this article’s verification date. The European Parliament and Council may amend the text before adoption, and transition provisions would then determine when any new regime replaces the current one.

## The control problem behind SFDR

Product disclosure depends on information flowing from investee companies, data vendors, estimates and portfolio systems into legal documents and marketing material. That creates a familiar governance question: can each public claim be traced to a binding investment rule, a calculation and a review control?

The most serious weakness is not a missing adjective. It is inconsistency: a prospectus promises one approach, the website describes another, the portfolio system cannot test either, and the periodic report quietly uses a third methodology.

Treating SFDR as a controlled data-and-claims process is more durable than treating it as a classification exercise—especially while the legislation is being revised.
