Ask a reporting team where their electricity emission factor comes from, and you will usually get a confident answer. Ask who selected the methodology and source, which vintage of the factor was used, when that decision was made, and why it was appropriate, and the answer is often less clear.

This is not necessarily negligence. It is what happens when a decision gets made once, under time pressure, by somebody who then moves on, and the decision quietly becomes infrastructure.

## Why factors drift out of ownership

An emission factor may look like a constant in a calculation file. The decision to use that factor is not.

For Scope 2 electricity, the accounting method comes first. Location-based reporting generally relies on grid-average emission factors. Market-based reporting uses qualifying contractual information and, depending on what is available, may use supplier-specific rates, residual-mix factors or permitted fallback data. Where the GHG Protocol's dual-reporting requirements apply, companies report both location-based and market-based results.

Within that framework there are still judgments to make: which source is appropriate, which geography it represents, which reporting period or data vintage it relates to, whether it meets the relevant quality criteria, and whether a more appropriate source is available. A UK Government conversion factor and an AIB residual-mix factor, for example, are not interchangeable datasets. They serve different purposes within different accounting contexts.

Once those decisions are made, the selected inputs get embedded in a calculation file and start producing numbers.
From that point, three things tend to happen.

The person who made the choice changes role. The rationale, which may have lived in their head or in an email, goes with them. The calculation stays.

The file gets copied forward each reporting cycle because rebuilding it is expensive and the existing one works. Each copy inherits the methodology, source and assumptions without necessarily re-examining them.

Then the calculation becomes load-bearing. Several years later, discovering that a stale or inappropriate factor has been used can trigger a much bigger question: whether historical figures or the base year need to be recalculated, whether comparative information remains meaningful, and how the change should be explained.

There is an important distinction here. Good governance does not mean freezing the numerical value of a factor forever. The opposite is often true. Grid factors, residual mixes, and other published factors can legitimately change from year to year as the underlying system, data, and methodology change.

The objective is not to keep the factor unchanged.

It is to keep the methodology controlled, the inputs current, and the history intact.

> A factor that nobody can defend is not a constant. It is an unexamined assumption with a decimal point.

## What this costs

For a while, potentially nothing. The numbers reconcile, the trend looks plausible, and nobody asks where the input came from.
The cost arrives when somebody does.

An assurance provider may ask for the basis of a material factor: what source was used, whether it was appropriate to the reporting period and methodology, and why it was selected. A customer performing supplier due diligence may want to understand whether your figure is comparable with another supplier's. Management may ask why emissions moved materially from one year to the next.

Without factor history, that last question can be surprisingly difficult to answer.

A change in reported electricity emissions can reflect a change in consumption. It can reflect a real change in the carbon intensity of the grid or supplier mix. It can reflect a change in contractual instruments. It can also reflect a new data vintage, a revised source dataset, a correction, or a methodological change in the factor itself.

If you have not preserved the factor history, separating those effects becomes reconstruction rather than analysis.
At that point you are not defending a number. You are trying to reconstruct a decision from incomplete records, usually against a deadline, which is the most expensive time to do it.

There is a subtler internal cost as well.

Different factor types across sites do not automatically invalidate a group inventory. In a market-based inventory, for example, differences in available contractual data can legitimately lead to different inputs at different sites.

But those differences can make site-to-site performance comparisons misleading.

If one site's result is driven by supplier-specific information while another relies on a residual mix or permitted fallback, management cannot assume the difference between them is purely operational. Part of the apparent performance gap may come from the accounting basis or the quality and type of data available.

That does not mean the numbers are wrong. It means the basis needs to travel with the number.

## The fix is unglamorous

Nothing about the remedy is technically difficult. It is a register.

For each material factor in use, record the value and unit, the accounting method it supports, the factor type, the applicable site or geography, the source and exact version, the underlying data or vintage year, the publication date, the reporting period for which it is being used, who selected or approved it, when, why it was considered appropriate, and when it should next be reviewed.

Publication year alone is not enough. A factor published in 2026 may be based on earlier underlying data, and the period it is appropriate to apply it to may be different again.

For factors linked to contractual instruments, the register should also identify the relevant evidence and whether the applicable quality criteria have been considered. When a factor changes, preserve the previous version and its effective period rather than overwriting it.
A few practical points matter disproportionately.

**Register the exceptions explicitly. If a site deviates from the group methodology or default source, document why. The problem is rarely that exceptions exist. It is that, several years later, nobody can explain them.

**Record supersessions rather than overwriting. When a factor changes, keep the previous value, source and effective period. If a methodology changes materially, record that separately from an ordinary annual change in the numerical factor. This makes later recalculation assessments and trend explanations far easier.

**Preserve the evidence used, not only the URL. Published datasets are revised, corrected and reorganised. A live link may not reproduce the version used in the original calculation. Retain the source artefact, version or other immutable evidence where licensing and systems permit it, together with the retrieval date.

**Use review triggers, not just review dates. An annual review is useful, but some events should trigger review immediately: a new dataset release, a methodological revision by the publisher, a change of electricity supplier or contractual instrument, an acquisition, a reporting-boundary change, or a change in the reporting standard being applied.

The register does not need to be sophisticated. Its job is to stop a methodological decision from becoming an undocumented inherited assumption.

Why it is worth doing now

Input provenance is already part of emissions reporting requirements, and it remains prominent in current standards-development work.
Under the current ESRS, undertakings reporting GHG emissions are required to disclose the methodologies, significant assumptions and emission factors used to calculate or measure those emissions, together with the reasons why they were chosen.

GHG Protocol's March 2026 Scope 3 progress update points in a similar direction. Among the draft revisions under development are additional requirements to disaggregate reported emissions by data type in order to improve transparency over the information underlying the inventory.
That March document is not a public consultation, and the proposals are not yet a final standard. Following the July 2026 decision by GHG Protocol and ISO to consolidate their corporate carbon-accounting standards, an integrated public consultation on the future corporate standard is planned for Q2 2027.

There is a parallel assurance point in Europe. Where CSRD sustainability reporting is mandatory, that reporting is subject to a limited-assurance opinion, and EU-level limited-assurance standards are now due to be adopted by 1 July 2027.

None of those requirements says: maintain an emission-factor register.

That is not the point.

The register is a control that makes the underlying requirement easier to satisfy. When a material number depends on a factor, you should be able to show what was used, which methodology and period it belongs to, where it came from, whether it was current and appropriate, and why the choice was defensible.

That is the same underlying question I keep coming back to in [almost everything I write about reporting](https://firatbarca.com/blog/from-guesswork-to-audit-trails/): can you show where this came from?
An emission-factor register is a small artefact. For a modest inventory, it can often be started in an afternoon, and once it is part of the reporting process the maintenance burden is small.

More importantly, it changes the nature of the conversation.

Instead of saying, “this is the factor we have always used,” you can show which factor was used, for which period, under which methodology, who approved it, what replaced it, and why.

That is a much more defensible place to be when somebody competent starts asking.
