Ask a reporting team where their electricity emission factor comes from and you will usually get a confident answer. Ask who selected it, in which year, and on what basis the choice was made over the alternatives, and the room often goes quiet.

This is not negligence. It is what happens when a decision gets made once, under time pressure, by somebody who then moves on, and the decision quietly becomes infrastructure.

## Why factors drift out of ownership

An emission factor is a judgment dressed as a constant. Choosing between a supplier-specific factor, a residual mix, a national grid average, or a published dataset such as DEFRA or AIB is a methodological decision with a defensible rationale behind it. Once selected, the factor gets embedded in a calculation file and starts producing numbers.

From that point, three things tend to happen.

The person who made the choice changes role. The rationale, which lived in their head and possibly in an email, goes with them. The factor stays.

The calculation file gets copied forward each cycle, because rebuilding it is expensive and the existing one works. Each copy inherits the factor without re-examining it.

The factor becomes load-bearing. By year four, the trend line depends on it, and changing it would create a restatement conversation nobody wants to have. The cost of revisiting the decision now exceeds the cost of leaving it alone, so it is left alone.

> A factor that nobody can defend is not a constant. It is an unexamined assumption with a decimal point.

## What this costs

For a while, nothing. The numbers are internally consistent, the trend is smooth, and no one asks.

The cost arrives when somebody does ask. An assurance provider wants the basis for the factor. A customer running supplier due diligence wants to know whether your figure is comparable to another supplier's. A year-on-year movement needs explaining and you cannot separate a real operational change from a factor artefact, because you do not know when the factor last changed.

At that point you are not defending a number. You are reconstructing a decision from incomplete records, usually against a deadline, which is the most expensive way to do it.

There is also a subtler cost. Inconsistent factor selection across sites produces group totals that cannot be meaningfully compared internally. If three countries use supplier-specific factors and the rest use national averages, aggregate performance mixes two different things, and the comparison between sites that management is drawing from it is not sound.

## The fix is unglamorous

Nothing about the remedy is technically difficult. It is a register.

For each factor in use: the value, the source and its publication year, who selected it, when, the alternatives considered, the reason for the choice, and the date it should next be reviewed. One row per factor.

That last field, the review date, is the one most often left out and the one that does the most work. Without it, a register is a snapshot that ages silently. With it, the register generates its own maintenance.

A few practical notes from doing this:

**Register the exceptions explicitly.** Sites that deviate from the group default need a documented reason. Undocumented deviation is the pattern that causes the most trouble later.

**Record supersessions rather than overwriting.** When a factor changes, keep the old value with its effective dates. This is what makes restatements explainable rather than mysterious.

**Attach the source file, not a reference to it.** Published datasets get revised and reorganised. A link that resolved in 2023 may not resolve in 2027.

## Why it is worth doing now

The direction of travel in emissions reporting is towards evidencing the quality of the inputs, not just the correctness of the output. The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 revision, published as a progress update in March 2026, is consulting on separating reported emissions into tiers by data type. CSRD requires limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application, and EU-level limited assurance standards are due by July 2027.

Both of those point at the same underlying question, which is the one I keep coming back to in [almost everything I write about reporting](/blog/from-guesswork-to-audit-trails/): can you show where this came from.

An emission factor register is a small artefact. It takes an afternoon to start and a few hours a year to maintain. It is also, in my experience, the single fastest way to convert a set of numbers that feel solid into a set of numbers you can actually defend when somebody competent starts asking.
