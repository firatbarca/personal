The EU Taxonomy is a classification system for environmentally sustainable economic activities. It does not award a general green label to an entire company, and it does not decide whether every investment is ethically or socially desirable. It answers a narrower question: under defined technical criteria, which economic activities can be treated as environmentally sustainable?

The distinction between **Taxonomy-eligible** and **Taxonomy-aligned** activity is central. Eligibility means an activity is described in the Taxonomy’s delegated acts and can be assessed. Alignment means the activity passes all the applicable tests. An eligible activity can have zero alignment.

## The essential terminology

**Economic activity.** A process through which inputs are combined to produce goods or services. Taxonomy assessment happens at activity level, not by assigning one undifferentiated status to the whole legal entity.

**Taxonomy-eligible.** An activity that is covered by a description in the relevant Taxonomy delegated act, whether or not it meets the technical criteria.

**Taxonomy-aligned.** An eligible activity that satisfies all four conditions in Article 3 of the Taxonomy Regulation.

**Substantial contribution.** The activity meets the criteria for making a meaningful contribution to at least one of the six environmental objectives.

**Do no significant harm (DNSH).** While contributing to one objective, the activity must not significantly harm any of the other environmental objectives.

**Minimum safeguards.** Procedures designed to align the activity with specified international standards on responsible business conduct and human rights, including the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.

**Technical screening criteria.** Detailed, activity-specific performance requirements adopted through delegated acts. These may contain quantitative thresholds, qualitative conditions or both.

## The six environmental objectives and four tests

The Taxonomy Regulation establishes six objectives:

1. climate-change mitigation;
2. climate-change adaptation;
3. sustainable use and protection of water and marine resources;
4. transition to a circular economy;
5. pollution prevention and control; and
6. protection and restoration of biodiversity and ecosystems.

For an economic activity to be Taxonomy-aligned, it must:

1. contribute substantially to at least one objective;
2. do no significant harm to the others;
3. be carried out in compliance with minimum safeguards; and
4. comply with the applicable technical screening criteria.

This is why “covered by the Taxonomy” is not a claim of environmental performance. Coverage only opens the door to assessment.

## Turnover, CapEx and OpEx tell different stories

Non-financial companies within the applicable disclosure scope report three core **key performance indicators (KPIs)**:

- **turnover** associated with eligible and aligned activities;
- **capital expenditure (CapEx)** associated with relevant assets, processes or qualifying plans; and
- **operating expenditure (OpEx)** within the specific definition used by the disclosure rules.

Turnover indicates the share of current revenue connected with covered or aligned activities. CapEx can show investment in the future direction of the business, including eligible plans to bring activities into alignment when the regulatory conditions are met. OpEx is not simply the company’s total operating expenses; the delegated act uses a narrower denominator covering specified categories of expenditure.

A company can therefore have low aligned turnover and higher aligned CapEx without contradiction. That may indicate a transition under way—but only if the CapEx plan and technical criteria genuinely support that conclusion.

**Double counting** occurs when the same amount is allocated more than once within a KPI. Preventing it requires an activity-level mapping linked back to the financial records. The percentages are only as reliable as the bridge between the Taxonomy assessment and the accounting data.

## Scope and the 2026 simplification

Taxonomy disclosure obligations are linked to the EU corporate-reporting framework. Omnibus I narrowed mandatory CSRD reporting from financial year 2027 to undertakings exceeding both 1,000 employees and €450 million net turnover. This brings the headline CSRD threshold into line with the threshold used for mandatory Taxonomy reporting by non-financial undertakings, while other reporting can remain voluntary or arise through financial-product rules.

Scope should still be checked against the consolidated legislation and the company’s precise status. Financial undertakings use different Taxonomy indicators, such as the **Green Asset Ratio (GAR)** for parts of banking activity, rather than the non-financial company template.

The Taxonomy is also used outside corporate Article 8 disclosures. It appears in sustainable-finance product disclosures and the European Green Bond framework. The same term can therefore arise in different reporting contexts with different denominators and purposes.

## A reliable assessment process

Begin with the actual products, services and revenue streams rather than a list of legal entities. Map them to the descriptions and classification codes in the applicable delegated acts. Confirm eligibility before testing alignment. Then assess substantial contribution, DNSH and minimum safeguards separately, retaining evidence for every conclusion.

For each KPI, reconcile the denominator to the financial statements and document allocation keys used to calculate the numerator. Record activities that are eligible but not aligned, including which test failed or where evidence was insufficient.

> A zero aligned percentage can be a defensible result. An unexplained percentage cannot.

The Taxonomy is deliberately technical. A persuasive sustainability narrative does not compensate for a failed threshold, missing DNSH assessment or unreconciled denominator.
