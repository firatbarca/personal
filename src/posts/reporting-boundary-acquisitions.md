A company reports emissions down 8% year on year. During that year it divested a manufacturing division and acquired a services business.

How much of the 8% is performance?

Without boundary documentation, that question cannot be answered, and the reported improvement is a mixture of two effects that happen to point the same way. This is one of the more common sources of misleading sustainability trends, and it is rarely deliberate.

## Why boundaries drift quietly

Financial consolidation has decades of accounting infrastructure behind it. Acquisitions get an effective date, an opening balance, and a defined treatment of comparatives. Everyone involved knows the rules.

Emissions consolidation is often bolted onto that process afterwards, and the questions that were settled for financial purposes get answered again, informally, by whoever is compiling the inventory.

When did the acquired entity enter the boundary: acquisition date, start of the reporting period, or the point at which its data became usable? Those give three different answers and all three are defensible.

Are prior periods restated to include it? If yes, on what data, since the entity was not collecting on your basis before you owned it. If no, the trend compares different populations.

What happens to a divested entity's historical emissions? Removing them from prior years makes the trend comparable but changes numbers you already published. Leaving them in preserves the published record but breaks comparability going forward.

> An emissions trend across a changing portfolio is measuring two things at once. Unless you separate them, it is reporting neither clearly.

## The separation that fixes it

The technique is borrowed and it works well: report the change in components.

- **Organic change**, meaning the like-for-like movement in entities present in both periods
- **Acquisition effect**, the emissions added by entities that entered the boundary
- **Divestment effect**, the emissions removed by entities that left
- **Methodology effect**, changes caused by revised factors or corrected calculations rather than by anything physical

Four numbers that sum to the reported movement. It takes more work than a single figure and it answers the question a reader actually has, which is whether the organisation is improving.

It also protects you in the other direction. A company whose emissions rose because it acquired a manufacturing business has a real story to tell about underlying performance, and without the decomposition it has no way to tell it credibly.

## What to document, and when

The recurring theme in [almost everything I write about reporting](/blog/from-guesswork-to-audit-trails/) applies here too: the cost of this is trivial at the time and substantial in retrospect.

At the point of any transaction, record the entity, the effective date used for emissions consolidation, the rationale for that date, the treatment of comparatives, the data source for the opening period, and any known gaps in the first year of reporting. One short entry per transaction, written when the corporate development team is still available to answer questions.

Three years later, that entry is what allows a restatement to be explained in a paragraph. Without it, someone is reading transaction documents to work out when a factory changed hands.

## Why this is becoming more visible

Two developments make boundary discipline harder to defer.

CSRD requires limited assurance over sustainability reporting from the first year of application, with EU-level limited assurance standards due to be adopted by July 2027. Consolidation boundary is exactly the sort of item an assurance provider examines, because it is high impact and often poorly evidenced.

The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 Phase 1 Progress Update, published in March 2026, is consulting on a coverage floor with justified exclusions. Coverage requires a defined universe of what should be included, and an entity acquired eighteen months ago that never entered the boundary is a completeness gap regardless of whether anyone has noticed it.

## The underlying point

Portfolio change is normal. No organisation should distort its commercial decisions to protect the readability of an emissions trend, and I am not suggesting otherwise.

What is avoidable is publishing a trend that conflates ownership change with performance, and then being unable to separate them when someone asks. That is not a measurement failure. It is a documentation failure, and it is one of the cheapest ones to prevent and one of the more expensive ones to repair.
