Every tonne of freight that moves generates an emissions figure that at least two organisations need. The carrier reports it as Scope 1. The customer reports the same movement as Scope 3, category four, upstream transportation and distribution. One physical journey, two inventories, two sets of expectations about how precisely it should be measured.

That overlap is a useful place to stand if you want to understand why Scope 3 is difficult.

## The number is not the hard part

For a given shipment, the calculation is reasonably well understood. Distance, mode, weight, load factor, fuel type, an emission factor. The methodology exists and the sector has spent years converging on it.

What is hard is that the number has to cross an organisational boundary and stay intact.

Inside a single company, a figure travels through systems you control. You can trace it, correct it, and reproduce it. Once it moves to a customer, all of that infrastructure stays behind. What arrives on the other side is a value in a spreadsheet, usually with no method attached, no version, and no way to reconcile it against anything.

I have written before about [the missing audit trail in ESG reporting](/blog/from-guesswork-to-audit-trails/). Scope 3 is that problem with an extra wall through the middle of it.

> A number that cannot survive the journey between two organisations is not really data. It is a claim.

## What the boundary actually costs

Three specific frictions show up repeatedly.

**Method mismatch.** Two parties can calculate the same movement correctly and still disagree, because they made different reasonable choices about allocation, load factor assumptions, or well-to-wheel versus tank-to-wheel scope. Neither is wrong. The gap still has to be explained to somebody.

**Timing mismatch.** The customer's reporting cycle rarely aligns with the carrier's. Requests arrive for periods that are already closed, at a level of granularity that was never retained.

**Granularity mismatch.** A customer wants shipment-level detail for their largest lanes and an annual total for everything else. Those are different products from the same underlying system, and building both is not free.

None of this is a reason to avoid the work. It is a reason to understand that supplier-specific Scope 3 data has a cost structure, and that cost sits with the supplier while the benefit lands with the customer. Any serious conversation about primary data has to acknowledge that asymmetry, because it explains most of the slow progress.

## Why this is about to matter more

The GHG Protocol published its Scope 3 Phase 1 Progress Update in March 2026, the first major rework since 2011. Among the proposals under discussion are a 95% coverage floor and the separation of reported emissions into tiers by data type, distinguishing specific, secondary, and spend-based figures. The final standard is targeted for late 2027 and the consultation is not complete, so the detail will move.

But if a version of tiering arrives, the request landing on carriers changes character. Today a customer asks for a number. Under tiering, the customer needs a number they can classify as primary, which means they need to know how it was produced. The question stops being "what is the figure" and becomes "what stands behind it."

That is a much better question. It is also considerably more work to answer at scale.

## What I have taken from it

Working on the supply side of somebody else's Scope 3 has changed how I read Scope 3 disclosures generally. When I see a well-populated upstream transport category, I now think about the reporting infrastructure of the carriers behind it, not just the diligence of the company publishing it. Good Scope 3 data is usually evidence of a relationship, not just a process.

It has also made me more sympathetic to companies with visible gaps and more sceptical of companies with none. Complete Scope 3 coverage across a diverse supplier base, achieved quickly and without visible seams, tends to mean the estimates are doing quiet work somewhere.

The organisations that will handle the next phase of this well are the ones treating supplier emissions data as a shared asset with two owners rather than a request one party makes and the other fulfils. That framing is slower to set up. It survives an auditor's questions considerably better.
