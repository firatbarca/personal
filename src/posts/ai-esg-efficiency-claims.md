A figure has been circulating in AI-for-ESG marketing material: that AI-powered platforms reduce ESG reporting effort by up to 90.8%, saving an average of 4.5 months of manual work annually.

I spend my working life asking where numbers come from. So I went looking for where this one came from, and I could not find a methodology behind it. No sample, no definition of "reporting effort," no baseline, no explanation of what the decimal place in 90.8% is doing.

That decimal place is the part that bothers me most. A single decimal implies a precision that a claim like this cannot possibly support. It is the sort of detail that makes a number feel measured rather than asserted.

## The test we already apply

In ESG controlling, we have a standard set of questions for any figure arriving from outside. They are not sophisticated, and they work:

- What is the population, and how was it selected?
- What is the baseline being compared against?
- Who produced the figure, and what outcome benefits them?
- Can the calculation be reproduced from stated inputs?

Run the 90.8% claim through those questions and very little survives. We do not know whose reporting effort was measured, what those organisations were doing before, whether the comparison is like for like, or whether the sample is a handful of enthusiastic early adopters.

> We would not accept this evidence for an emissions figure. It is not clear why we accept it for a procurement decision.

To be fair to the vendors, the underlying direction is not implausible. Document extraction, data consolidation, and first-draft narrative genuinely do compress with good tooling. I use these tools and they save me real time. My objection is not that AI is useless in ESG work. It is that an unfalsifiable number is being used to make a case that could be made honestly with weaker claims and better evidence.

## Where the time actually goes

The efficiency framing also misdescribes the work, which is a more interesting problem than the arithmetic.

In my experience, drafting is not where reporting cycles are lost. The time goes into:

- Chasing the three countries whose submission is incomplete
- Resolving why this quarter's figure moved 12% and whether the cause is real
- Deciding which emission factor applies and documenting why
- Reconciling a group total against forty-seven local ones that were each internally consistent

Almost none of that is text generation. Most of it is judgment, chasing, and reconciliation across organisational boundaries. An AI tool that drafts a disclosure section quickly has automated the visible tip of the work while leaving the expensive part untouched.

There is a version of the vendor claim that would be genuinely credible: that AI reduces the effort of a specific, named task by a measured amount under stated conditions. That claim is less impressive and considerably more useful.

## What good evidence would look like

If a vendor wants to convince a reporting function, the evidence is not hard to specify:

**Name the task.** "Time to produce a first draft of a narrative disclosure section" is measurable. "Reporting effort" is not.

**State the baseline.** Compared with what? A team with no tooling, or a team with a mature spreadsheet-based process? The gap between those two baselines is most of the claimed saving.

**Publish the sample.** How many organisations, in which sectors, at what maturity level.

**Report the failures.** Every process improvement has cases where it did not help. An evaluation with no negative results is a case study, not evidence.

I would find a well-documented 30% claim far more persuasive than an undocumented 90.8% one, and I suspect most people who work with data would say the same.

## Why this matters beyond procurement

I have argued before that [AI belongs in ESG reporting as an assistant rather than an author](/blog/ai-in-esg-reporting-assistant-not-author/). This is the commercial version of the same boundary.

Sustainability reporting spent a decade being criticised for numbers that could not be traced to a source. The profession has worked hard to fix that, and the fix has been genuinely painful. It would be an odd outcome if teams that now demand provenance for every tonne of CO2 accepted a headline efficiency statistic with no visible method behind it, simply because it appeared in a slide deck rather than a sustainability statement.

The discipline we built for our own numbers is worth pointing outward as well. Ask the vendor the question we ask ourselves every cycle: how do you know?
