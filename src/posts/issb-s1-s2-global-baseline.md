The International Sustainability Standards Board (**ISSB**) was created by the IFRS Foundation to develop a global baseline of sustainability-related financial disclosures for capital markets. Its first two standards are **IFRS S1 General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information** and **IFRS S2 Climate-related Disclosures**.

IFRS S1 supplies the general reporting architecture. IFRS S2 applies that architecture specifically to climate. Both were issued in June 2023 and are effective in the standards themselves for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024. That does not mean they automatically became law everywhere on that date. Each jurisdiction decides whether, when and how to adopt or otherwise use them.

## The terms to understand first

**Primary users.** Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors—the people who use general-purpose financial reports when deciding whether to provide resources to a company.

**Sustainability-related financial information.** Information about sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect cash flows, access to finance or cost of capital over the short, medium or long term.

**Material information.** Information is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence decisions primary users make on the basis of the company’s general-purpose financial reports.

**Global baseline.** A common investor-focused foundation that jurisdictions can adopt and supplement. It does not mean every jurisdiction will reproduce the standards word for word or use the same effective date.

**Physical climate risk.** Risk arising from climate-related events or longer-term climatic changes, such as flooding, heat stress, drought or sea-level rise. Physical risks may be acute events or chronic changes.

**Transition risk.** Risk arising from the move towards a lower-carbon economy, including changes in policy, law, technology, markets or reputation.

## What IFRS S1 requires

IFRS S1 asks a company to disclose material information about sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could affect its prospects. The disclosures are organised around four connected areas inherited from the TCFD architecture:

- **governance**—the processes, controls and oversight used to monitor and manage the matters;
- **strategy**—effects on the business model, value chain, decisions and financial outlook;
- **risk management**—how risks and opportunities are identified, assessed, prioritised and monitored; and
- **metrics and targets**—performance measures and progress against targets.

The **value chain** is the full range of interactions, resources and relationships connected with the company’s business model, from supply and production through distribution, use and end of life. A risk does not have to sit inside a consolidated legal entity to affect the reporting company’s prospects.

IFRS S1 also connects sustainability disclosures with the financial statements. They normally cover the same reporting entity and period and are published as part of the general-purpose financial reports at the same time as the related financial statements, subject to available first-year transition relief.

## What IFRS S2 adds

IFRS S2 requires disclosure of material climate-related physical risks, transition risks and opportunities. It includes information about climate resilience, transition planning, financial effects, emissions and industry-relevant metrics.

**Climate resilience** means the capacity of a strategy and business model to adjust to climate-related changes, developments and uncertainties. A company must use **climate-related scenario analysis** to inform its assessment. Scenario analysis is not a prediction of the future. It tests how the business might perform under plausible sets of assumptions. The approach should be proportionate to the company’s exposure and the skills, capabilities and resources available.

IFRS S2 requires reporting of greenhouse-gas emissions across three scopes:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by the company;
- **Scope 2:** indirect emissions from the generation of purchased or acquired electricity, steam, heating or cooling consumed by the company; and
- **Scope 3:** other indirect emissions in the value chain, divided by the GHG Protocol into 15 upstream and downstream categories.

The standard generally requires measurement using the GHG Protocol Corporate Standard. It recognises that Scope 3 frequently depends on estimates. The key control questions are therefore not only “What is the number?” but also “Which categories are included, what boundary was used, where did the data come from, and how uncertain is it?”

## ISSB, TCFD and ESRS are related—but not identical

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (**TCFD**) completed its work and disbanded in October 2023. Its four-part structure and recommendations were incorporated into the ISSB standards. A company may still refer to TCFD, or be required to do so under local rules, but IFRS S2 is the more current global standard built on that foundation.

ISSB materiality is investor-focused. ESRS use **double materiality**, adding the company’s impacts on people and the environment whether or not they are financially material. The financial-materiality lens in ESRS is designed to align closely with ISSB materiality, and the IFRS Foundation and EFRAG have published interoperability guidance. Still, applying one set does not automatically satisfy every requirement of the other.

The practical approach for a company facing both regimes is to build one controlled information base, identify common disclosures, then document the additional impact-oriented or jurisdiction-specific requirements separately.

## A sensible implementation sequence

Start by confirming the applicable jurisdictional rule and reporting entity. Map material sustainability-related risks and opportunities to the financial-planning and enterprise-risk processes. Identify where data definitions, periods or boundaries differ from the financial statements. Then build an evidence trail for the judgements, estimates, scenario analysis and metrics.

The hardest part is rarely the final narrative. It is connecting operational evidence, risk assessment and financial effects without contradicting the accounts or overclaiming precision.
