Somewhere in most reporting functions is a number that is known to be wrong.

Not fraudulently wrong. A boundary that was drawn incorrectly, a factor applied to the wrong denominator, a site that was double counted for two cycles before anyone noticed. It was published, it sits in the trend line, and correcting it means explaining it.

So it does not get corrected. The error is carried forward, consistently, because consistency at least looks like control.

I understand the reasoning and I think it is the wrong trade.

## Why the avoidance is rational in the short term

The fear is specific and not irrational. A restatement invites the question of what else is wrong. It disturbs a trend that may be attached to a target, a remuneration outcome, or a public commitment. It requires explaining a technical error to an audience that may not distinguish between a methodological correction and a credibility failure.

Against that, carrying the error forward has no immediate cost. Nobody is asking. The number is internally consistent.

The problem is that the cost is not zero, it is deferred and compounding. Every cycle the error persists, it becomes more expensive to correct, because there is more history to restate and a longer period during which the organisation knew and said nothing. That last part is what turns a technical issue into a credibility one.

> A correction you volunteer is a control working. A correction someone else finds is a control that did not.

## The elements of a restatement that lands well

Having seen this handled both ways, the difference is mostly in the construction.

**Lead with the cause, not the number.** Open with what went wrong and why, in plain language. Readers who understand the mechanism will accept the change. Readers given only a revised figure will assume the worst available explanation.

**Show both figures and the bridge.** Original, restated, and the components of the difference. A restatement without a reconciliation asks for trust at exactly the moment trust is under question.

**State the periods affected and restate all of them.** Correcting the current year while leaving prior years on the old basis produces a trend that is not comparable to itself, which is worse than the original error.

**Say what changed in the process.** This is the part most often omitted and it does most of the work. An error with no described control change reads as something that could recur. An error with a specific fix, such as a validation rule added or a review step introduced, reads as a process that improved.

**Do not bury it.** A restatement disclosed in a footnote and discovered by a reader is a considerably worse outcome than the same restatement disclosed clearly.

## Building for restatement before you need it

Most of the difficulty is created earlier, by not keeping the records that make a correction explainable.

An [emission factor register](/blog/emission-factor-nobody-owns/) that records supersessions with effective dates lets you say precisely when a value changed and what it was before. Without it, you are reconstructing.

Boundary documentation, recording which entities were in scope for which periods and why, is what lets you explain an acquisition-related restatement in a paragraph rather than a project.

Version control on calculation files, even something as simple as dated copies with a change note, is what distinguishes a correction you can trace from one you can only assert.

None of this is expensive to maintain. All of it is expensive to recreate under pressure with a publication deadline approaching.

## What the direction of travel implies

The trend in reporting requirements is towards more evidence about how figures were produced. CSRD requires limited assurance from the first year of application, with EU-level limited assurance standards due by July 2027. The GHG Protocol's Scope 3 revision is consulting on separating reported emissions by data type. Both push in the same direction, which is towards figures that carry their own provenance.

In that environment, errors become more likely to be found rather than less. The organisations that will handle this comfortably are the ones that have already normalised correction as part of the process rather than treating it as an event.

Financial reporting worked this out a long time ago. Restatements happen, there is a standard way to present them, and a well-handled one damages credibility considerably less than a concealed error discovered later.

Sustainability reporting is young enough that the norm is still being set. It would be a good outcome if it settled on the version where correcting yourself is evidence that the system works.
