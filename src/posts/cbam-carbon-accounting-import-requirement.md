The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (**CBAM**) puts a carbon-related obligation on imports of specified emissions-intensive goods. After a reporting-only transitional period that began in October 2023, the definitive regime started on 1 January 2026.

CBAM is not a conventional customs tariff with one fixed rate. An authorised declarant reports the **embedded emissions** in covered imports and surrenders CBAM certificates. The certificate price is linked to the price of allowances in the EU Emissions Trading System (**EU ETS**), with adjustments where an eligible carbon price has already been paid in the country of production.

## The terminology that drives the mechanism

**Carbon leakage.** The risk that carbon-intensive production moves to jurisdictions with less stringent climate policies, or that EU production is displaced by more emissions-intensive imports.

**Embedded emissions.** Greenhouse-gas emissions released during production of the imported goods, calculated under the CBAM rules. Depending on the goods and applicable methodology, the calculation can include direct emissions and specified indirect emissions.

**Direct emissions.** Emissions from production processes over which the installation has direct control, including relevant heating and cooling consumed in production under the regulatory definition.

**Indirect emissions.** Emissions associated with production of electricity consumed during the production process, where the CBAM rules require them to be included.

**Authorised CBAM declarant.** The importer or eligible indirect customs representative authorised by the competent authority to import relevant goods and fulfil the declaration and certificate obligations.

**CBAM certificate.** An electronic certificate corresponding to one tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent embedded in covered goods. It is a compliance instrument, not a general-purpose voluntary carbon credit.

**EU ETS allowance.** A tradable compliance unit under the EU Emissions Trading System permitting the emission of one tonne of carbon-dioxide equivalent. CBAM certificate prices are linked to EU ETS auction prices, but CBAM certificates and ETS allowances are not the same instrument.

## Which goods and importers are covered?

CBAM currently covers selected goods in six sectors:

- cement;
- iron and steel;
- aluminium;
- fertilisers;
- electricity; and
- hydrogen.

Legal scope is determined by the customs classification codes and rules in the Regulation, not by the product’s everyday commercial name. Some downstream products are included, and exclusions apply in specified circumstances.

Following the 2025 simplification, the Commission describes a single mass-based threshold: EU importers or indirect customs representatives importing more than 50 tonnes of CBAM goods must generally obtain authorised-declarant status. Electricity and hydrogen are treated under specific rules, so the threshold should not be applied mechanically to every import. Importers also need to consider anti-circumvention rules and the aggregation of imports.

An **indirect customs representative** acts in its own name but on behalf of another person for customs purposes. Whether the importer or representative carries the CBAM responsibility depends on establishment, representation and authorisation conditions.

## How the definitive regime works

The process connects customs, installation-level emissions data and an annual financial obligation.

First, the declarant identifies covered imports and the installations that produced them. Second, embedded emissions are calculated using the prescribed methodology and, where required, verified by an accredited verifier. Third, the declarant submits the annual CBAM declaration through the CBAM Registry. Finally, it surrenders the required number of certificates after taking account of eligible carbon prices paid abroad and the adjustment reflecting free EU ETS allocation.

For emissions embedded in goods imported during 2026, the Commission uses a quarterly average of EU ETS auction closing prices to determine certificate prices. From 2027, the general pricing frequency changes to a weekly average. The financial effect therefore depends on both emissions intensity and the linked ETS price.

**Actual emissions** are calculated from installation data under the regulatory methodology. **Default values** are prescribed fallback values used where permitted. Defaults can be operationally convenient, but they may not reflect a producer’s real performance and should not be assumed to produce the lowest obligation.

## Why this is a supply-chain data problem

The EU importer has the compliance obligation, while much of the required production data originates at a non-EU installation. Contracts, supplier onboarding and data controls therefore matter as much as the calculation formula.

A useful evidence pack includes:

- customs codes, quantities and country of origin;
- the producing installation and production route;
- source data for fuel, process and electricity emissions;
- allocation methods where an installation produces multiple goods;
- verifier reports where verification is required;
- evidence of any carbon price paid in the country of origin; and
- reconciliations between customs records, the CBAM Registry and certificate calculations.

The **production route** is the technology and sequence of processes used to manufacture the good. Two products with the same customs code can have different embedded emissions because their production routes, fuels, electricity sources and efficiency differ.

## CBAM is related to—but different from corporate Scope 3

Both systems may use supplier data, but CBAM embedded emissions are a regulatory product calculation with prescribed boundaries. Corporate **Scope 3 emissions** are indirect value-chain emissions reported under corporate greenhouse-gas accounting. The same purchase may appear in both systems, but one result should not be copied into the other without checking boundaries, gases, allocation and methodology.

CBAM also complements the EU ETS. It is designed to align the carbon treatment of covered imports with EU production as free allocation under the ETS is phased down. It does not replace the ETS and does not apply one global carbon price to all imported products.

The practical shift is important: carbon data are no longer only part of an annual sustainability narrative. For covered imports, they affect authorisation, customs processes, verified declarations and a certificate obligation.
